How much does car insurance go up after a ticket?

The best-supported national figure is this: a single speeding ticket raises US premiums by roughly 20–25% on average at renewal; reckless driving and DUI commonly raise them 60–110% (Governors Highway Safety Association). That is the number we lead with, and it is an average, not a promise for your policy.

The increase is a percentage of your own premium. We do not publish a single dollar amount for the increase because base premiums differ widely by driver, vehicle, insurer and state. The same percentage adds more dollars to a higher premium and fewer to a lower one.

Where you land inside or outside the range depends on several things:

  • Your insurer's own rating rules and filed rates.
  • Your state's rules on surcharges and what insurers may consider.
  • Your prior record. A clean record often means a different result than a record with earlier tickets or claims.
  • The violation itself. Speeding is treated differently from reckless driving or DUI.

Think of the 20–25% figure as a planning estimate for one ordinary speeding conviction. It is not a quote. The only way to know your number is to ask your insurer or compare quotes after the conviction appears on your record.

All figures on this page are national averages. They vary by insurer and state, so treat them as a starting point rather than a prediction.

How long does a ticket affect my insurance?

Insurers typically look back 3–5 years of motor-vehicle-record history. A ticket can therefore influence pricing for that span, although the effect may shrink as the violation ages and other factors change.

Timing matters in a second way. The increase applies at renewal after the conviction reaches the record. Getting a ticket does not usually change your premium that day. The change shows up when your policy renews and the insurer reviews your record.

That sequence has practical consequences:

  • A ticket issued today may not touch your rate until your next renewal.
  • If the case is still open, there may be no conviction on the record yet, and the pricing effect may not have started.
  • The lookback window is set by each insurer. Some use the shorter end of the 3–5 year span and some the longer end.

Because lookback periods differ, we do not tell readers that a surcharge ends on a particular date. Your insurer can tell you how far back it reviews and when a violation is expected to stop counting.

One useful habit is to note the date of the conviction and ask at each renewal whether the violation is still being rated. If you shop for a new policy, a different insurer may weigh the same record differently.

Which violations raise insurance the most?

Serious violations raise premiums far more than ordinary speeding. Reckless driving and DUI commonly raise premiums 60–110%, compared with roughly 20–25% for a single speeding ticket (Governors Highway Safety Association).

The reason is risk pricing. Insurers treat behavior that signals a higher chance of a crash or a costly claim as a stronger reason to raise the rate. Speed is a well-documented factor in crash severity, which is why speeding itself is rated at all. See the safety background from NHTSA and IIHS.

In rough order from least to most expensive for your premium:

  1. Minor non-moving or equipment tickets, which often cause no increase.
  2. A single speeding ticket, at roughly 20–25% on average.
  3. Reckless driving and DUI, at 60–110%.

These are averages, and where a specific offense falls depends on the insurer and the state. Some states also add their own surcharges or point systems on top of what insurers charge, which we cover below.

If you have more than one violation in the lookback window, expect the combined effect to be larger than a single ticket. We do not give a formula for stacking, because insurers apply their own rules.

Does every ticket raise insurance rates?

No. Minor non-moving or equipment tickets often cause no increase. Examples include a broken tail light or an expired registration sticker, depending on how the insurer and state classify them.

Moving violations are the ones that usually matter, because they are tied to how you drive. Even then, the effect is not the same everywhere. Some states restrict surcharges for a first minor violation. In those states an insurer may be limited in how it can price a first minor ticket.

Three points to keep in mind:

  • Classification matters. Ask how the violation is coded on your record, not just what the citation said.
  • State law matters. Restrictions on first-violation surcharges are not universal, and their details differ by state.
  • Conviction matters. The increase applies at renewal after the conviction reaches the record, so the outcome of the case affects the result.

We do not list which states have first-violation limits here, because we only publish what we can source. For rules that apply to you, check with your state insurance regulator or the state resources linked through the Governors Highway Safety Association.

Not every ticket is rated the same way. A non-moving or equipment ticket and a speeding conviction can have very different effects on the same policy.

Do New Jersey and North Carolina add their own costs?

Yes. A few states layer their own systems on top of ordinary insurer pricing, and two well-known examples are New Jersey and North Carolina.

New Jersey. The state bills separate MVC surcharges ($150+/yr for 3 years at 6+ points) on top of insurer pricing. This charge comes from the state, not from your insurance company. Your insurer may still raise your premium separately.

North Carolina. The state's SDIP assigns insurance points with rate increases of 30%+ per point tier. In other words, the more points assigned for your violations, the higher the tier and the larger the increase.

What these two examples show is that the national average is only part of the picture. In some states the cost has two layers:

  • The price your insurer charges at renewal.
  • A state-set surcharge or point-based rate rule.

If you live in or are cited in a state like these, read that state's rules before assuming the national average applies. We give only the sourced figures above and do not estimate other state-specific amounts. Your state motor vehicle agency or insurance department is the right place to confirm current rules.

Can I keep a ticket from raising my insurance?

Sometimes. Because the increase applies at renewal after the conviction reaches the record, the outcome of your case can affect your premium. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and cannot give legal advice, but here are the general options people look at.

  • Contest the ticket. If you are not convicted, there may be no conviction to rate. Whether that is realistic depends on the facts and your court.
  • Ask about reduction or alternatives. Some courts offer reduced charges or other outcomes. Availability varies by state, county and court, and so do any fees. We do not state a fee because it is not something we can verify for your court.
  • Check first-violation protections. Some states restrict surcharges for a first minor violation.
  • Compare insurers at renewal. Insurers weigh the same record differently, so quotes can differ.
  • Ask about lookback. Insurers typically review 3–5 years of record history, so ask when the violation is expected to stop counting.

Before agreeing to any outcome, ask the court or a licensed attorney in your state how it would appear on your record. An outcome that lowers the fine but still counts as a conviction may not help your premium.

Remember that a minor non-moving or equipment ticket often causes no increase, so it is usually not worth treating it like a speeding conviction.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore a ticket?

Ignoring a ticket usually makes things worse, and the details depend on your state and court. Common consequences in many places include added penalties, a hold on your license or registration, or a warrant. We do not give exact amounts or deadlines here because they vary by county and court.

There is also an insurance angle. A ticket that ends in a conviction can reach your record and be rated at renewal. If you let a case lapse, the result may be a conviction by default or a suspension, and either can affect how an insurer prices you.

Practical steps:

  1. Read the citation for the response deadline and court contact details.
  2. Contact the court clerk if you are unsure what your options are.
  3. Keep records of anything you pay or file.
  4. If the stakes are high, such as a reckless driving or DUI charge, consider speaking with a licensed attorney in your state.

Reckless driving and DUI commonly raise premiums 60–110%, so these charges deserve particular care. We cannot recommend a specific attorney or insurer.

Frequently asked questions

How much does insurance go up after a speeding ticket?

A single speeding ticket raises US premiums by roughly 20–25% on average at renewal. That is a national average, so your result may be higher or lower depending on your insurer and state. Reckless driving and DUI commonly raise premiums 60–110%.

How long does a speeding ticket stay on my insurance?

Insurers typically look back 3–5 years of motor-vehicle-record history, so a ticket can affect pricing for that span. The exact period depends on the insurer and state. Ask your insurer how far back it reviews and when the violation should stop counting.

When does my premium go up after a ticket?

The increase applies at renewal after the conviction reaches your record. Your premium does not usually change on the day you receive the ticket. If the case is still open and no conviction is recorded, the pricing effect may not have started.

Does a non-moving or equipment ticket raise my insurance?

Minor non-moving or equipment tickets often cause no increase. Examples might include a broken light or an expired sticker, depending on how it is classified. Rules differ by insurer and state, so confirm with your insurer how your violation is coded.

Does a DUI or reckless driving charge raise rates more than speeding?

Yes. Reckless driving and DUI commonly raise premiums 60–110%, compared with roughly 20–25% for a single speeding ticket. These are national averages. The actual increase varies by insurer and state, and by the rest of your record.

Do states add their own surcharges on top of insurance increases?

Some do. New Jersey bills separate MVC surcharges ($150+/yr for 3 years at 6+ points) on top of insurer pricing. North Carolina's SDIP assigns insurance points with rate increases of 30%+ per point tier. Check your own state's rules.

Can a first minor ticket be treated differently?

In some places, yes. Some states restrict surcharges for a first minor violation. The rules differ by state and are not universal, so check with your state insurance regulator or your insurer to see whether a restriction applies to you.
