How much is a texting / cell phone ticket in Pennsylvania?

Nationally, a first-offense texting or cell phone ticket costs $20–$500 by state. In Pennsylvania the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services).

We do not list an exact Pennsylvania dollar figure on this page. The range above is a national range, and the number that applies to you is the one printed on your citation or stated by the court.

Bottom line: treat $20–$500 as a national yardstick only. Your citation and the court that handles it control the Pennsylvania amount.

The fine is only part of the bill. Court costs, the EMS surcharge and other statutory fees typically exceed the base fine, so the amount you actually pay can be well above the fine line alone.

How is the fine set, and why is the total higher than the fine?

In Pennsylvania, the fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. That means it is not a single number you can look up for every driver and every county. You can browse the code itself through Pennsylvania statutes via Justia.

On top of the fine, a conviction usually brings several add-ons:

  • Court costs
  • An EMS surcharge
  • Other statutory fees

Taken together, these typically exceed the base fine. If you are budgeting, read the total due on the citation or court notice rather than the fine alone.

Because the amount is set by the court, two drivers with similar tickets can see different totals. If the paperwork is unclear, the court office listed on the citation can tell you what is owed and how it breaks down.

How many points does a texting / cell phone ticket carry in Pennsylvania?

The Pennsylvania points information we have locked is written around speeding. It says 2–5 points for 11+ mph over, and that 6 points triggers a departmental exam/hearing. High-speed convictions add a 15-day suspension at 31+ over.

Those speeding thresholds do not tell you the point value for a texting or cell phone conviction. We are not stating one here, because we do not have a sourced figure for it.

To find out whether your citation carries points, check these places:

  1. The violation description and statute listed on your citation.
  2. Your driver record and points information from PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services.
  3. The text of the traffic code on Justia.

Why it matters: the 6-point threshold is where a departmental exam/hearing is triggered. If you already have points from other violations, ask how a new conviction would affect your total.

Does a texting / cell phone ticket raise insurance in Pennsylvania?

It can. Nationally, moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Pennsylvania-specific one.

Whether a particular conviction changes your rate depends on your insurer, your driving history and how the violation is recorded. We do not recommend any insurer and cannot predict your premium.

A practical approach is to ask your insurer how they treat a conviction of this kind at your next renewal. Also ask whether an outcome other than a conviction would be treated differently, since the wording of the final disposition can matter.

The insurance effect is separate from the fine. Even a modest fine can cost more over time if it leads to a higher premium.

What are my options after getting a texting / cell phone ticket?

Most citations give you a few paths. The deadlines and instructions are printed on the ticket, so read it soon after you get it.

  • Pay it. This usually resolves the matter, but payment generally means accepting the conviction and its consequences, including any points and the insurance effect.
  • Contest it. You can ask for a hearing and present your side to the court.
  • Ask the court about alternatives. Some courts discuss payment arrangements or other resolutions. Availability varies by court, so ask the clerk.

Because the Pennsylvania fine is set by the court, the amount you face may depend on how the case is resolved. Court costs, the EMS surcharge and other statutory fees typically exceed the base fine, so compare total cost, not just the fine.

This page is general information, not legal advice. If the stakes are high for you, such as a license already near the 6-point threshold, consider speaking with a licensed attorney of your choosing.

Can a texting / cell phone ticket be dismissed or reduced?

It is possible, but outcomes depend on the facts and on the court. We do not have sourced statistics on how often tickets of this kind are dismissed or reduced in Pennsylvania, so we will not suggest a likelihood.

If you contest the ticket, these steps tend to help:

  1. Check the citation for errors, such as the wrong date, location, vehicle or statute.
  2. Note the deadline to respond, and meet it.
  3. Gather anything that bears on what happened, such as notes written soon after the stop.
  4. Attend the hearing on time and be prepared to explain your side.

Even a reduced outcome can still carry court costs, the EMS surcharge and other statutory fees. Ask how those apply if the charge changes, because the total may not fall as much as the fine does.

What happens if I don't pay or respond?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. The court and PennDOT can take further steps when a ticket goes unanswered, and the deadlines are set out on the citation and any follow-up notices.

Since the fine, costs and fees are set by the court, an unpaid balance can grow or lead to additional court action. The exact consequences depend on your case, so we are not stating specific penalties here.

If you cannot pay by the due date, contact the court listed on your citation before the deadline and ask what is available.

For driver-license questions, PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services is the official place to check your record and status.
