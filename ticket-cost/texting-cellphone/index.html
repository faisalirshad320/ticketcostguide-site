How much is a texting or cell phone ticket in the US?

The best single number for a first offense is $20–$500 for a first offense by state, with repeat-offense escalation (Governors Highway Safety Association). That is a very wide band, and it exists because each state writes its own penalty.

Some states treat a first phone violation as a low-level infraction with a small base fine. Others set a much higher penalty from the start. In nearly every state, a second or later offense costs more than the first.

The amount printed on your citation may not be the full amount you pay. Several pieces can sit on top of the base fine:

  • The base fine set by the state statute or fine schedule.
  • Court costs and administrative fees.
  • Surcharges or assessments added by the state or county.
  • Any extra penalty for a repeat offense.

Because those add-ons differ by county and court, the total you owe varies by county or court. We do not publish an exact local figure, and you should not rely on any site that does without showing its source.

Your citation and the court's posted fine schedule are the final word on what you owe. The ranges on this page are for planning, not a quote.

A texting ticket is usually a moving violation. That is why the cost question often turns into a second question about points and insurance, which we cover below.

How much does a texting ticket cost by state?

Only some states have locked figures on this page. For every other state, the honest answer is that it varies by state, and you should check your own state's statute and fine schedule.

Here are the two states we can put numbers on.

  • California: the base fine is $20 for a first offense and $50 for a subsequent offense. Once assessments are added, totals reach ≈$160+.
  • New York: fines run $50–$200 for a first offense, and each handheld conviction adds 5 points.

The California example shows why a base fine alone can mislead. A $20 base fine sounds minor, but the assessments added on top push the total to ≈$160+. The exact total varies by county and court.

The New York example shows the opposite problem. The fine range is moderate, but the points are what many drivers care about, since they follow your license record.

Both examples sit inside the national range of $20–$500 for a first offense by state (Governors Highway Safety Association). If your state is not listed here, expect your first-offense amount to fall somewhere in that band, with escalation for repeats.

To find your state's exact rule, search your state's vehicle code through a public code library such as State codes via Justia. Then compare it with the fine schedule posted by the court named on your ticket.

Do not assume a neighboring state's fine applies to you. Penalties are set state by state and sometimes court by court.

How many points does a texting ticket add to your license?

Points depend on the state. We have one locked example: in New York, each handheld conviction adds 5 points.

For other states, the point value varies by state. Some states assign points for phone violations, and some do not. We will not guess at a number.

A few things are worth knowing about how points generally work:

  • Points are normally added when you are convicted, not when you are cited.
  • Paying the ticket without contesting it is usually treated as a conviction.
  • Points can add up with other violations and may trigger a state review of your license.

Those are general patterns, not a rule for every state. The thresholds that lead to a suspension or a required course vary by state, so check with your state's motor vehicle agency.

In New York, 5 points per conviction is meaningful because points from several tickets can accumulate. We do not state New York's suspension threshold here because it is not one of our locked figures.

If points matter to you, read the back of the citation and your state's point schedule before paying. Paying often counts as an admission.

TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and cannot tell you whether a specific outcome is right for your case. A court clerk or your state's licensing agency can confirm how a conviction would be recorded.

Does a texting ticket raise your car insurance?

It can, but the effect varies by insurer and by state. We have no locked percentage or dollar figure for the increase, so we will not give one.

Insurers generally check your motor vehicle record when you apply and when you renew. A conviction for a moving violation may show up there. What the insurer does with it is its own decision, within the limits of state law.

These factors tend to shape the outcome:

  • Whether the violation ends up as a conviction on your record.
  • Your prior driving history and any other recent tickets.
  • Your insurer's own rating rules.
  • State rules on how insurers may use violations.

Some states let drivers keep a violation off their record through a court-approved option. Whether that is available for a phone ticket varies by state and by court.

If you are worried about a rate increase, the useful step is to ask your insurer how it treats a phone-use violation. TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer or tell you which company to choose.

The fine is a one-time cost. An insurance increase can last across several renewals, so it is worth weighing both before you decide how to respond to the ticket.

Repeat offenses can matter more. Because fines escalate for repeats, and records build up, a second phone ticket may cost more in the fine and in the long run.

Is handheld phone use illegal in every state?

Almost. Nearly every state now bans texting while driving, and most ban handheld use outright. States with a full handheld ban are often called hands-free states (Governors Highway Safety Association).

That distinction matters for what you can be ticketed for. In a texting-only state, the violation usually involves reading, writing or sending messages. In a hands-free state, simply holding the phone can be enough.

The details vary by state, including:

  • Whether the ban covers all handheld use or only texting.
  • Whether a voice call on a hand-held phone is allowed.
  • Whether using a phone at a red light counts as driving.
  • Whether the offense is a primary or secondary one, meaning whether an officer can stop you for it alone.

We have not given specific rules for those points because they are not in our locked facts. Your state's statute is the place to check, and a public code library such as State codes via Justia is a reasonable starting point.

Laws change often. A rule that applied last year may have been tightened, so confirm the current text for your state.

If you drive across state lines, the law of the state where you are driving applies. A handheld call that is fine at home may be a ticket next door.

Can a texting ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes. Whether it can in your case varies by state, by court and by the facts. TicketCostGuide cannot give legal advice or say what your odds are.

These are the general routes people ask about:

  • Contesting the ticket: you plead not guilty and the court hears the case. The officer's evidence has to support the violation.
  • Asking for a reduction: some courts allow a plea to a lesser violation, which may carry a lower fine or fewer consequences.
  • Court-approved programs: some courts offer ways to keep a violation off your record. Availability varies by state and court.
  • Fixing a paperwork problem: errors on a citation can matter, but whether they lead to dismissal depends on local rules.

Phone cases can be hard to prove either way, since the question is often what the driver was doing with the device. A hands-free setup, a navigation app or a phone in a mount may or may not fit an exception, depending on state law.

If you decide to contest, read the instructions on the citation. They state how to respond and where. Missing a deadline can remove your options.

If you want advice on a specific case, speak to a licensed attorney in your state. We do not recommend any particular attorney.

Remember that paying the fine is often treated as a plea of guilty. In a state that assigns points, as New York does with 5 points per handheld conviction, that choice has consequences beyond the money.

What happens if I don't pay a texting ticket?

Ignoring a ticket usually makes things worse. The exact consequences and deadlines vary by state and court, and we have no locked figures for late penalties.

Common outcomes of an unpaid or ignored ticket include:

  • Late fees or additional penalties added to the original amount.
  • A default conviction, which may add points where the state uses them.
  • A hold or suspension on your license until the matter is resolved.
  • A warrant in some places, depending on the court and the type of offense.

Not every state does all of these. Some courts send reminders first, and some move straight to penalties. The notice you received, or the court's website, will explain what applies to you.

Because the fine can already escalate with repeat offenses, adding late penalties on top can push the total well above the amount you first saw. In California, for example, assessments already lift a $20 base fine to ≈$160+ in total, before any late costs.

If you cannot afford the amount, contact the court clerk. Many courts offer payment plans or other arrangements, though availability varies by court.

Do not wait until the deadline passes. Contacting the court early is almost always better than letting the ticket default.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a ticket for texting and driving?

A first offense typically runs $20–$500 depending on the state, with higher fines for repeat offenses. The final amount varies by state, and by county or court once assessments and fees are added. Check your citation and the court's fine schedule for the exact total.

How much is a cell phone ticket in California?

California's base fine is $20 for a first offense and $50 for a subsequent offense. With assessments added, totals reach ≈$160+. The exact total varies by county and court, so check your citation or the court's schedule for the amount you owe.

How many points is a handheld cell phone ticket in New York?

New York adds 5 points per handheld conviction. Fines for a first offense run $50–$200. Points are normally added on conviction, so how you respond to the ticket matters. Other states vary, and some assign different point values.

Is it illegal to hold a phone while driving in every state?

Nearly every state bans texting while driving, and most ban handheld use outright, which makes them hands-free states. The exact rules vary by state, including what counts as use and whether an officer can stop you for it alone. Check your state's statute.

Will a texting ticket raise my insurance?

It can, but the effect varies by insurer and by state. We have no locked percentage or dollar figure for an increase. Insurers often review your driving record, so ask your own insurer how it treats a phone-use violation.

Can I get a texting ticket dismissed?

Sometimes, but it varies by state, court and the facts of the stop. Options can include contesting the ticket, asking for a reduction or using a court-approved program. TicketCostGuide gives no legal advice, so consider speaking with a licensed attorney in your state.

Do texting fines go up for a second offense?

Yes, in general. The national first-offense range of $20–$500 comes with repeat-offense escalation. California shows this clearly, with a $20 base fine for a first offense and $50 for a subsequent offense. The exact increase varies by state.
