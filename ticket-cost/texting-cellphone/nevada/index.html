How much is a texting / cell phone ticket in Nevada?

Nationally, a first-offense texting or cell phone ticket falls between $20 and $500, depending on the state. In Nevada, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Nevada Department of Motor Vehicles).

This page does not give a single Nevada dollar figure, because the amount depends on the court that handles your case. The $20–$500 range is a national comparison. It is not a Nevada price list.

The number that matters is the one printed on your citation or shown by the court that issued it. Administrative fees may be added on top of the fine.

If you want a rough sense of scale, the national range shows how widely states differ. Some set modest first-offense fines and others set much higher ones. Nevada's amount can only be confirmed through the court.

How is the fine set, and what else gets added?

The fine for this violation in Nevada is set by the court within the state's traffic code. Statutes are searchable through Nevada statutes via Justia. The court, not the officer, determines the final amount.

On top of the fine, administrative fees are added by the court. These amounts vary by jurisdiction. Two drivers cited for the same behavior in different courts can owe different totals.

When you review your citation, look for:

  • The court name and its contact details.
  • The listed fine or the instruction to appear.
  • Any line items for administrative fees.
  • The payment or response deadline.

If the total is unclear, ask the court clerk for an itemized amount before paying. A clerk can explain the charges but cannot give legal advice.

How many points does a texting / cell phone ticket carry in Nevada?

Nevada's points system assigns 1–5 demerit points, scaled by mph over for speed violations. A texting or cell phone violation does not involve speed, so we do not state a point value for it here.

Check your citation and the Nevada Department of Motor Vehicles to see how a texting conviction is scored on your record.

Threshold to know: 12 points in 12 months = a 6-month suspension.

If you already have points from other violations, even a small addition could matter. Request your driving record from the DMV so you know where you stand before deciding how to respond to a citation.

The points question is also why a conviction can cost more than the fine itself. Points stay on your record and can feed into the suspension threshold and into how insurers view you.

Does a texting / cell phone ticket raise insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Nevada-specific quote.

Your own change could be higher, lower, or nothing at all. It depends on your insurer, your record, and whether the ticket ends in a conviction.

Things that commonly affect the outcome:

  • Whether the citation results in a conviction on your record.
  • Other violations or claims already on your driving history.
  • Your insurer's own rating rules and when your policy renews.

TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer or give insurance advice. If you want a precise answer, ask your insurer how a moving violation would affect your renewal.

Can a texting / cell phone ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but the outcome depends on the court and the facts of your case. We cannot say whether your ticket qualifies. Nevada's traffic code sets the rules, and the court on your citation decides what applies.

In general, a citation gives you a few ways to respond:

  1. Pay the fine. This is usually treated as accepting the violation, which may mean a conviction and any points that go with it.
  2. Contest it. You ask the court to hear the case, where the evidence is reviewed.
  3. Ask the court about alternatives. Some courts offer other resolutions. Whether yours does, and whether you are eligible, is the court's call.

Before choosing, compare the cost of paying with the possible effect on your record and insurance. A conviction can carry consequences beyond the fine, including points and a possible premium increase of about 20% nationally.

For personal guidance on a contested case, you would need to speak with a licensed attorney. We do not recommend specific attorneys.

What happens if I don't pay or respond?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can add administrative fees, and amounts vary by jurisdiction. The court may also take further action on your case or your license.

We do not state exact late penalties here because they depend on the court. The citation or the court clerk can tell you the deadline and what happens after it passes.

If you cannot pay the full amount or will miss the deadline, contact the court clerk before the date passes. Courts are often easier to work with before a deadline than after.

Unresolved tickets can also affect your driving record. If your record already carries points, remember that 12 points in 12 months = a 6-month suspension.
