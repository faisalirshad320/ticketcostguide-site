How much is a texting ticket in Florida?

The lead figure is a national one: nationally $20–$500 for a first offense by state; in Florida the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (FLHSMV). That range shows how widely states differ. It is not the Florida fine.

We do not state an exact Florida dollar figure on this page. The amount for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code, and the number printed on your citation or on the clerk's payment notice is the one that counts.

Quick rule: treat $20–$500 as a national range, and treat the amount on your own citation as your Florida amount.

The base fine is also only part of what you pay. County courts add costs and fees on top of it, which is covered below.

How is the fine set, and what gets added on top?

In Florida the court sets the exact amount for this violation within the state's traffic code. The statutes are published online through Florida statutes via Justia, and the state's driver-licensing agency is FLHSMV.

Beyond the statutory penalty, county courts add costs and fees. These vary by county and court, so two drivers with the same violation can owe different totals.

School and work-zone amounts are doubled by statute. If your citation notes that the stop happened in one of those zones, expect a higher figure than a standard stop.

Things to check on your citation or the clerk's notice:

  • The base amount for the violation
  • Any listed court costs or fees
  • Whether a school or work zone is noted
  • The payment or court-appearance deadline

How many points does a texting ticket add in Florida?

Florida's points system, as we have it from our sources, is 3 points (15 or less over) or 4 points (16+ over). Those tiers are written in terms of speed over the limit, so they describe the speeding schedule. They do not map neatly onto a texting citation.

We therefore do not assign a specific point value to a texting ticket. How your citation is scored is something to confirm with the clerk of court or with FLHSMV.

The threshold that matters across all violations is this: 12 points in 12 months = 30-day suspension. Points from a texting ticket, if any are assessed, add to whatever else is on your record in that window.

If you already have points from other tickets, check your driving record before deciding how to resolve this one.

Does a texting ticket raise car insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by ~20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Florida-specific one.

Your own change depends on your insurer, your record, and whether the ticket ends in a conviction. We do not name or recommend insurers. If the premium effect worries you, ask your insurer how it treats citations and how long they stay on your rating.

When weighing options, add the possible premium increase to the fine and court costs. A lower upfront payment is not always the cheaper path overall.

Can a texting ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Possibly, but the outcome is up to the court. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and does not give legal advice, so this is a general outline of the usual choices.

  • Pay the citation. This generally resolves it, but it may count as a conviction. That affects points and insurance.
  • Contest it. You can ask for a hearing and present your side. The court decides.
  • Ask about other options. The clerk of court can explain what is available for your specific citation and county.

Because the exact amount is set by the court and county costs vary, ask the clerk for the total before you choose. If you want advice on a specific case, a licensed attorney is the right person to ask.

For how states treat distracted-driving laws more broadly, see the Governors Highway Safety Association state laws page.

What happens if I don't pay a texting ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Missing a payment or court deadline can lead to added costs and to driver-license consequences. We do not state specific penalty amounts or timelines because our sources do not give them.

Points also matter here. Reaching 12 points in 12 months means a 30-day suspension, so an unresolved ticket that adds points could push you toward that threshold.

If you cannot pay by the deadline, contact the clerk of court listed on your citation before it passes. Ask what is possible in your county. Keep a record of what you were told and when.

What should I check before deciding what to do?

A few minutes with the paperwork can save money. Use this order:

  1. Read the amount and deadline on the citation or clerk's notice.
  2. Ask whether county court costs and fees are included in that total.
  3. Check whether a school or work zone is noted, since those amounts are doubled by statute.
  4. Ask how points are assessed for your citation, and compare that with your current record against the 12-points-in-12-months threshold.
  5. Think about your insurance. A conviction typically raises premiums by about 20% on average nationally.

None of this replaces legal advice. It helps you compare the total cost of each option before you choose.
