How much is a texting or cell phone ticket in New Hampshire?

Nationally $20–$500 for a first offense by state; in New Hampshire the exact amount is set by the court on the citation — Texting / Cell Phone Ticket cost in New Hampshire (New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles).

The $20–$500 range is a national picture. It shows how widely states differ, and it is not a New Hampshire price list. We do not publish a single New Hampshire dollar figure because the court sets it.

The most reliable number is the one printed on your citation or the court notice you received. That figure is what you would owe if you resolve the ticket as written.

Treat the national $20–$500 range as context only. Your New Hampshire amount comes from the court handling your citation.

How is the fine set, and what gets added to it?

In New Hampshire the court sets the exact fine within the state's traffic code. The base fine is the starting point, and other charges are added on top.

By statute, a 24% penalty assessment is added to every base fine. That means the amount you pay is higher than the base fine alone.

The state's traffic statutes are published online (New Hampshire statutes via Justia). Check them if you want to read the language that governs the offense you were charged with.

Because the court sets the fine, two drivers cited for similar conduct may see different totals depending on the court, the charge written on the citation and the facts of the stop.

  • Base fine: set by the court on the citation.
  • Penalty assessment: 24% added to every base fine.
  • Total owed: the base fine plus the assessment, as shown by the court.

How many points does a texting or cell phone ticket carry?

New Hampshire's points system assigns 3–4 points by offense. Which value applies to you depends on the specific offense listed on your citation.

Points matter because they add up. Under the locked state rule, 12 points in 12 months means up to a 3-month suspension.

A single texting ticket will not reach that threshold on its own. But if you already have other moving violations on your record, the points from this one could bring you closer to it.

Points are a separate consequence from the fine. Paying the ticket does not remove them, and contesting the ticket is the stage at which the charge itself can be addressed.

The DMV is the place to confirm your current point total and how your record looks (New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles).

Does a texting ticket raise my insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a New Hampshire-specific one.

Your actual change depends on your insurer, your driving history and how the violation appears on your record. Some drivers see little change, and others see more.

The insurance cost can add up over time, since a higher premium may stay in place for several renewals. For many drivers it ends up larger than the fine itself.

We do not recommend insurers or predict what a specific company will do. If you are concerned, ask your insurer how it treats a conviction of this kind.

Can a texting ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes the outcome can change, but that depends on the facts and on the court. We are not a law firm and cannot say whether an option fits your situation.

In general, a driver who receives a citation can choose among a few paths:

  • Pay the ticket. This usually resolves the case but may mean a conviction, points and insurance effects.
  • Contest the charge. You can ask the court for a hearing and present your side.
  • Ask the court what is available. The court clerk can explain procedures and deadlines for your citation.

Read the citation closely. It shows the charge, the court that handles it and the instructions for responding.

If you want advice on strategy, consider speaking with a licensed attorney of your own choosing. This site does not recommend any specific attorney.

What happens if I don't pay or respond?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can add consequences when a ticket goes unanswered, and the DMV can act on the record the court reports.

Because the exact fine is set by the court and carries a 24% penalty assessment on the base fine, delay does not lower what you owe. It can only add risk.

If you cannot pay the full amount or cannot make a court date, contact the court named on the citation before the deadline it lists. Courts handle these requests directly, and the citation will tell you how to reach them.

A missed response can also put your license status at risk. Check the instructions on your citation and with the DMV.
