How much is a texting or cell phone ticket in Washington?

Nationally, first-offense texting and cell phone tickets run $20–$500 depending on the state (Washington State Department of Licensing). That is a national range, not a Washington price.

In Washington, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation. We do not publish a single Washington dollar figure here because the number you owe is the one printed on your ticket or on the court's notice.

Bottom line: use the national range only as context. Your citation and the court that issued it decide what you owe.

If the amount on your paperwork looks very different from the national range, that does not by itself mean there is an error. Check that the violation listed matches what the officer cited, then confirm the total with the court.

How is the fine for a cell phone ticket set in Washington?

The court sets the amount within the state's traffic code. Washington traffic infractions are handled under the IRLJ (Infraction Rules for Courts of Limited Jurisdiction), and the court's schedule drives the amount on your citation.

Statutory assessments are built into the IRLJ schedule amounts. In practice, that means the figure you see is meant to be the whole amount for the infraction, not a base fine with a long list of extras added later.

  • Look at the citation for the violation description and the amount.
  • Check the court's notice for how and where to respond.
  • Ask the court clerk if the total on a payment page differs from the citation.

The statutes are published online through Washington statutes via Justia if you want to read the traffic code yourself. Court staff can explain how the schedule works but cannot give legal advice.

How many points does a texting or cell phone ticket add in Washington?

None, because Washington has no point system. Suspensions follow repeat moving-violation convictions within set windows (Washington State Department of Licensing).

So the question to ask is not how many points you will get, but how many moving-violation convictions are on your record and how close together they are. One ticket on its own is different from several in a short period.

Tip: you can request your driving record from the Department of Licensing to see which convictions are listed and when they were recorded.

We do not list the exact windows or thresholds here, because they are not part of the figures we have verified for this page. Check the Department of Licensing site or the statutes for the current rules before assuming how a new conviction would affect your license.

Does a texting or cell phone ticket raise insurance rates?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Washington-specific one.

Your own change could be higher, lower, or nothing at all. It depends on your insurer, your driving history, and whether the violation is reported as a conviction on your record.

  • Insurers usually review your record at renewal.
  • A conviction is more likely to matter than a ticket that was dismissed.
  • An earlier violation on your record can make a new one weigh more.

We do not recommend any insurer or advise on policy decisions. If you are worried about a premium change, ask your insurer how it treats moving violations before you decide how to respond to the citation.

Can a texting or cell phone ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but that is decided by the court, not by this guide. Most courts give you more than one way to respond to a citation: pay it, contest it, or ask for a hearing to explain the circumstances.

The citation or the court's notice lists your choices and the deadline to respond. Read it as soon as you receive it, since the choices can be limited once the deadline passes.

  • Paying usually means accepting the infraction, which can lead to a conviction on your record.
  • Contesting means asking the court to decide whether the violation happened.
  • Asking for a hearing on the circumstances means admitting the violation but explaining why, and the court decides whether to change the amount.

Because a conviction can affect insurance and repeat-offense suspensions, it is worth understanding the choice before you pay. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and cannot tell you which option fits your situation.

What happens if I don't pay a texting or cell phone ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can treat a missed response as a failure to respond, which can lead to added costs, a collections referral, or action involving your driver's license.

The exact consequences and timing are set by the court and the state, and they are listed on your citation or follow-up notice. We do not state specific late fees here, because we have no verified figure for them.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court before the deadline. Many courts can explain whether a payment arrangement is available.

An unpaid ticket can also become a bigger problem than the original violation, particularly if it triggers a license issue on top of a possible repeat-conviction suspension.
