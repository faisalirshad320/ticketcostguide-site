How much is a no insurance ticket in South Carolina?

Nationally, a no insurance ticket runs $100–$1,500+ by state, plus possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences. In South Carolina the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (South Carolina DMV).

That means no single South Carolina price applies to every driver. The amount can differ by county and by court, and this page does not state an exact dollar figure because the sourced facts do not support one.

The $100–$1,500+ range is a national spread across all states. It is not a South Carolina range, so treat it as context only.

The number that matters is the one printed on your citation or on the court's notice. Check it against the court's own payment instructions.

Beyond the fine itself, expect statewide assessments and surcharges. Those are covered below and often change the total more than the fine does.

How is the no insurance fine set in South Carolina?

The fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The code is published online, including through South Carolina statutes via Justia, and the DMV explains how insurance requirements connect to registration and driving privileges.

Because the court sets the figure, two drivers cited in different counties may see different amounts. We do not publish a state average, since none is in our sourced facts.

Your citation or court notice should show one of two things: an amount you can pay, or an instruction to contact or appear before the court. If it is unclear, ask the court clerk. Clerks can explain procedure but cannot give legal advice.

  • Read the citation for the court name, the date and any listed amount.
  • Confirm the payment method and deadline with that specific court.
  • Keep a copy of everything you send or receive.

Why does the total cost more than the fine?

Statewide assessments and surcharges are added to every fine in South Carolina, and they usually exceed it. So the amount you actually pay can be well above the base fine.

This is why a quoted fine alone can mislead. Always ask the court for the total due, not just the fine line.

Rule of thumb: the fine is only part of the bill. Ask for the full amount owed, including every assessment and surcharge.

Other costs may sit outside the court bill. The national picture includes possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences, and those can bring their own fees and paperwork. Our sourced facts give no South Carolina dollar amounts for them, so check with the DMV.

How many points is a no insurance ticket in South Carolina?

South Carolina's points system assigns 2–6 points by mph over the limit, and 12 points = suspension. Points halve after 12 months (South Carolina DMV).

That mph scale describes speeding violations. Our sourced facts do not state a point value for a no insurance citation, so we do not give one.

Confirm with the DMV or the court whether your specific citation carries points. The answer may depend on the exact charge on your ticket.

If points do apply, two things matter:

  • Total: points from all violations add up toward the 12-point suspension threshold.
  • Time: points halve after 12 months, so older violations count for less.

If you already have points from other tickets, ask the DMV for your current total before deciding how to handle this citation.

Does a no insurance ticket raise your insurance rates?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a South Carolina one, and your own change could be higher, lower or none.

It is also unclear whether insurers treat every no insurance citation as a moving violation. Rating rules differ by company, so ask your insurer how a conviction would be treated. TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer.

A lapse in coverage can create separate problems. Nationally, a no insurance violation can lead to an SR-22 requirement, which is a certificate of financial responsibility filed by an insurer. Whether and for how long South Carolina would require one in your case is something to confirm with the DMV.

The ~20% figure is an average for moving-violation convictions across the US. Do not read it as a prediction for your policy.

Can a no insurance ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes courts handle these citations differently depending on the facts, but we cannot promise any outcome. Whether a dismissal or reduction is available in South Carolina is up to the court that issued the citation.

These steps are worth considering:

  1. Read the citation closely for the exact charge, court and deadline.
  2. Check whether you had active coverage on the date of the stop. If you did, gather proof such as a policy declaration page.
  3. Ask the court clerk what documents the court accepts and whether you must appear.
  4. Ask the DMV how the citation affects your registration and license.
  5. If the stakes are high, consider speaking with a licensed South Carolina attorney. TicketCostGuide does not recommend specific attorneys.

Do not ignore the deadline while you gather paperwork. If you need more time, contact the court before the date passes.

Paying the citation generally resolves the charge, but it may also count as a conviction. Understand that before you pay.

What happens if I don't pay a no insurance ticket?

Unpaid traffic citations can lead to further court action, added costs and possible licensing or registration problems. The exact steps in South Carolina depend on the court and the DMV.

Because statewide assessments and surcharges are already part of the total, delay can leave you owing more than you expected. Ask the court what happens after a missed deadline.

Separate from the citation, driving without coverage can bring the national consequences noted earlier: possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences. Resolve your coverage first so a second citation does not follow the first.

  • Get active coverage in place before driving again.
  • Contact the court before the due date if you cannot pay in full.
  • Ask the DMV about registration status and any reinstatement steps.
