How much is a no insurance ticket in New York?

Nationally, a no insurance ticket costs $100–$1,500+ by state, plus possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences. In New York the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (New York State Department of Motor Vehicles).

New York allows fines up to $1,500 plus a $750 civil penalty for driving uninsured. That is the ceiling the state allows, not a fixed price for every driver.

Two other charges sit on top of the court fine: the $88–$93 mandatory surcharge and the DMV's Driver Responsibility Assessment. Your real total is the fine, plus those add-ons, plus any civil penalty.

We do not list a single New York fine amount, because the court sets it. Read the citation or your court notice for your number.

How is the fine set?

The fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. It is not a flat figure that every driver pays.

The court may look at the details of the citation and your record. We cannot say how any particular judge or court will weigh those details.

The citation or court notice is the document that tells you what you owe. If it shows a range or a court date instead of a final amount, the court or the DMV can confirm what applies.

  • Fine: set by the court, with New York allowing up to $1,500.
  • Civil penalty: New York allows $750 for driving uninsured.
  • Surcharge: $88–$93, added to the fine.

How many points does a no insurance ticket carry in New York?

The point schedule we have on file is built around speeding: 3 points (1–10 over), 4 points (11–20), 6 points (21–30), 8 points (31–40), and 11 points (41+).

Those tiers apply to speed-based violations. We do not list a separate point value for an uninsured-vehicle charge, so check your citation and the New York State Department of Motor Vehicles for how your charge is treated.

Points matter because of two thresholds. Reaching 11 points in 18 months means a suspension. Reaching 6+ points also triggers the separate Driver Responsibility Assessment, which is $300+.

If you already have points from other tickets, a new conviction could push you toward those thresholds.

What other charges come on top of the fine?

Two charges are added to the court fine. The first is the mandatory surcharge of $88–$93. The second is the DMV's Driver Responsibility Assessment, which starts at $300+ and applies at 6+ points.

The assessment is separate from the court. It comes from the DMV, so paying the court does not settle it.

A no insurance case can also bring non-dollar consequences nationally, including possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences. Whether and how these apply in your case depends on the citation and the DMV.

Add up the fine, the $750 civil penalty if it applies, the $88–$93 surcharge and any $300+ assessment before deciding how to respond.

Does a no insurance ticket raise my insurance?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a New York-specific one.

We do not have data on how a no insurance conviction in particular is priced. Insurers set their own rates, and a lapse in coverage can matter in its own right.

An SR-22, where required, is a filing that proves you carry coverage. It is one of the national consequences that can follow an uninsured-driving case, and a licensed insurer or the DMV can explain how it works for you.

We do not recommend any specific insurer. Comparing quotes from several companies is the usual way to see what you would pay.

Can a no insurance ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes the court has discretion, but we cannot promise any result. The fine is set by the court within the state's traffic code, so the outcome depends on the court and the facts of your citation.

Things worth checking first:

  • Whether the citation lists the correct vehicle, date and plate.
  • Whether you had valid coverage that can be documented.
  • The response deadline and the options printed on the notice.

Contacting the court clerk can tell you what your court accepts. You may also talk to a licensed attorney if you want advice on your situation. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and does not give legal advice.

What happens if I don't pay or respond?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse, and the details depend on the court and the DMV. Nationally, no insurance cases can involve impound, SR-22 and registration consequences, and a suspension is possible when points thresholds are reached.

The amounts can also grow in practice, because the fine, the $88–$93 surcharge and any DMV assessment are separate charges. We do not list late fee amounts, so check your notice.

If you cannot pay in full, ask the court clerk what options exist. Responding by the deadline keeps more choices open than staying silent.
