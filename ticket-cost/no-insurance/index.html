How much is a no insurance ticket in the US?

The lead figure for driving without insurance is $100–$1,500+ by state, plus possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences (Governors Highway Safety Association). The range is wide because every state writes its own penalty schedule.

The fine is only one part of the bill. Courts often add assessments, surcharges and administrative fees on top of the base fine. Those add-ons vary by state, county and court, so we do not give a single local number.

Beyond the court bill, a conviction can bring several other costs:

  • Possible impound. Some states allow the vehicle to be towed or held, which can mean towing and storage charges.
  • An SR-22 filing. Many states require this after conviction, and it raises insurance costs for ~3 years.
  • Registration consequences. Your registration can be affected until you show you are insured again.

Where you land in the range usually depends on your state, whether it is a first offense, and how the court treats the case. Judges and clerks in some places have room to reduce the amount, especially when you can show proof of coverage.

The $100–$1,500+ figure is a national range, not a quote. Your actual total depends on your state's schedule and your local court's added fees.

How much is a no insurance ticket in Texas, California and New York?

Three states show how different the penalties can be. These are the figures we can source, and we have kept them exactly as published.

  • Texas: fines of $175–$350 for a first offense.
  • California: the first-offense base fine is $100–$200 before assessments.
  • New York: fines up to $1,500 plus a civil penalty of $750 for driving uninsured.

The California figure is a base fine. Assessments and fees are added by the court, so the amount you actually pay is higher than the base. We cannot give an exact total because it varies by county and court.

The Texas range is for a first offense only. A repeat offense is treated differently, and the amounts for later offenses vary by state, so check your state's schedule.

New York stands out because it pairs a fine with a separate civil penalty. That is why its combined exposure sits at the top of the national range.

If your state is not listed here, look up its vehicle code. State codes are available through State codes via Justia, and the court or DMV that issued your ticket can confirm the current schedule.

A court's fine schedule, a state statute and a collections notice can each show different totals. Always confirm the amount due with the court that handles your citation.

How many points does a no insurance ticket add to my license?

This varies by state. We do not state a point value here because we have no sourced figure that applies across states.

Some states assess license points for a no insurance conviction. Others handle it mainly through fines, registration action or license consequences rather than points. A few treat it as an administrative matter with the DMV instead of a moving violation.

Here is what to check on your own ticket and your state DMV site:

  1. Whether the offense is listed as a moving or non-moving violation.
  2. Whether your state's point system covers it at all.
  3. Whether a conviction triggers a separate DMV action, such as a registration or license consequence.
  4. Whether the points, if any, stay on your record or drop off after a set period.

Even when a state adds no points, the conviction itself can still appear on your driving record. Insurers look at that record when they price a policy.

If you are unsure, ask the court clerk or the DMV how a conviction would be recorded before you decide to pay or contest the ticket. Paying is usually treated as a plea of guilty or no contest, which can have record consequences.

No point figure is published on this page because point values vary by state. Use your state DMV's official point schedule for the exact number.

Does a no insurance ticket raise my car insurance rates?

It often does. The biggest driver is the SR-22 requirement. Many states require an SR-22 filing after conviction, which raises insurance costs for ~3 years.

An SR-22 is not a type of insurance. It is a certificate your insurer files with the state to show you carry the required coverage. Insurers that offer it typically treat you as higher risk, so premiums tend to go up.

Other things that can affect your rates:

  • The conviction on your record. Insurers review driving records when you apply or renew.
  • A gap in coverage. A lapse can make some insurers price you as higher risk, even before any ticket.
  • Your state's rules. Not every state uses an SR-22, and what it requires varies.

We do not give a dollar figure for the rate increase. It varies by state, insurer and driving history, and we have no sourced number for it.

TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer. If you need coverage after a ticket, compare quotes from several companies and ask directly whether they file SR-22 forms in your state.

If your state requires an SR-22, keep continuous coverage for the whole period. A new lapse during that time can bring further penalties, and the details vary by state.

Can a no insurance ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Often, yes, when the problem is proof rather than coverage. If insurance existed at the stop but wasn't shown, most states dismiss or reduce the charge with proof.

This is the most common situation. A driver has a valid policy but cannot produce the card, or the officer's database check fails to find it. The fix is usually to show the court evidence that you were covered on the date of the stop.

Documents that may help:

  • An insurance declarations page that shows the policy period.
  • An insurance card valid on the date of the stop.
  • A letter from your insurer confirming coverage on that date.

Whether the court dismisses the charge entirely or reduces it varies by state and by court. Some courts may charge a small administrative fee even when they dismiss. We do not give a figure because it varies.

If you were not insured at the time of the stop, getting a policy afterward usually does not erase the violation. It can still matter, because some courts consider whether you have since obtained coverage when deciding on a reduction. That is up to the judge or the court's rules.

Deadlines for showing proof are set by the ticket and local court, so read your citation carefully and act before the date listed. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and cannot advise on your specific case. For legal questions, you can contact the court clerk or a licensed attorney of your choosing.

Do not ignore the ticket because you think proof will fix it. Missing the court's deadline can turn a dismissible ticket into a larger problem.

What happens if I don't pay a no insurance ticket?

Ignoring the ticket usually makes the situation worse. The details vary by state, county and court, but the general pattern is similar.

Courts commonly respond to an unpaid or unanswered citation by adding late fees or penalties, which raises the amount you owe above the original fine. They may also notify the DMV.

Possible consequences include:

  • Additional fees on top of the original fine.
  • Registration consequences, since the national range already includes possible registration consequences (Governors Highway Safety Association).
  • A license hold or suspension in some states.
  • A warrant or collections referral in some places.

Because the no insurance penalty can include possible impound, continuing to drive the vehicle while it is uninsured and the ticket is unresolved adds further risk. Another stop can mean a second citation.

If you cannot pay the full amount at once, ask the court whether it offers a payment plan or an extension. Many courts do, but the options vary by court. It is better to ask before the due date than after.

We cannot tell you the exact late fee because it varies by state, county and court. The citation or the court's website should list what applies to you.

What should I do right after getting a no insurance ticket?

The steps below can help you avoid extra costs, whatever your state. They are general guidance, not legal advice.

  1. Read the citation. Find the violation listed, the court, and the date by which you must respond.
  2. Check whether you were actually covered. If you had a policy at the time of the stop, gather proof. Most states dismiss or reduce with proof in that case.
  3. Get insured if you are not. Driving uninsured again risks a repeat violation and, depending on the state, possible impound.
  4. Ask about SR-22. If your state requires it after conviction, ask your insurer how to file. It raises insurance costs for ~3 years.
  5. Contact the court. Ask about the total due, payment options and any proof-of-insurance process.

Before paying, ask how a conviction would be recorded. Payment is often treated as a plea, and the record consequences vary by state.

If the penalty is serious, such as a New York ticket with fines up to $1,500 plus a civil penalty of $750, you may want to talk to a licensed attorney of your choosing. We do not recommend specific attorneys.

Keep copies of everything: the ticket, your proof of insurance, any court receipt and any SR-22 paperwork. You may need them if the matter comes up again.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a no insurance ticket?

The US range is $100–$1,500+ by state, plus possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences. Examples: Texas fines $175–$350 for a first offense, and California's first-offense base fine is $100–$200 before assessments. Your exact total varies by state, county and court.

How much is a no insurance ticket in New York?

New York allows fines up to $1,500 plus a civil penalty of $750 for driving uninsured. That puts it at the top end of the national range. Added fees and any other consequences vary, so confirm the total with the court or DMV handling your case.

Can I get a no insurance ticket dismissed if I had insurance?

Usually yes. If insurance existed at the stop but wasn't shown, most states dismiss or reduce the charge with proof. A declarations page or insurer letter covering the date of the stop can help. Whether it is dismissed or reduced, and any fee, varies by state and court.

Will a no insurance ticket require an SR-22?

Many states require an SR-22 filing after conviction. It raises insurance costs for ~3 years. Not every state uses it, and the rules vary, so ask your state DMV or your insurer whether it applies to you.

How many points is a no insurance ticket?

It varies by state. Some states add license points for a no insurance conviction, while others rely on fines, registration action or other DMV consequences. Check your state DMV's point schedule or ask the court clerk before paying, since paying may count as a conviction.

What if I can't afford the fine for driving without insurance?

Contact the court before the due date and ask about payment plans or extensions. Options vary by court. Ignoring the ticket can add costs and registration consequences, which are already part of the $100–$1,500+ range, so asking early is usually better.

Can my car be impounded for no insurance?

Possible impound is part of the penalty range: $100–$1,500+ by state, plus possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences. Whether a vehicle is actually impounded depends on your state's rules and the circumstances of the stop.
