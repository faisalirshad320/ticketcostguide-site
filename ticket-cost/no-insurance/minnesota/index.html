How much is a no insurance ticket in Minnesota?

Nationally, a no insurance ticket costs $100–$1,500+ by state, plus possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences; in Minnesota the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Minnesota Department of Public Safety — Driver and Vehicle Services). That is the lead figure for this page, and we do not publish a made-up Minnesota dollar amount.

The national range is wide because states treat the offense very differently. Some charge a modest base fine. Others add large penalties, especially for repeat offenses or for a lapse in coverage.

The number that matters for your situation is the one printed on your citation or shown by the court that handles it. Treat the national range as context only.

On top of the fine, a statutory state surcharge is added to every payable fine. So the total you pay can be higher than the fine line alone.

How is the fine set in Minnesota?

The exact fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. It is not a single number that applies the same way everywhere, and it is not something a guide like this can calculate for you.

You can read the underlying law in the Minnesota statutes published on Justia (Minnesota statutes via Justia). Look in the traffic regulations chapter for the insurance requirement and its penalties.

  • Start with the citation. It lists the charge, the court and how to respond.
  • Check the court's instructions. The court clerk can tell you the payable amount and whether a surcharge is included.
  • Ask about total cost. The surcharge is added to every payable fine, so confirm the all-in figure before paying.

If anything on the citation is unclear, the court clerk is the right person to ask. We cannot give legal advice or tell you how a particular court will treat your case.

How many points does a no insurance ticket add in Minnesota?

Minnesota has no point system. License action follows the record of convictions and specific high-speed offenses, not a running point total.

That means you will not find a point value to look up for this offense. What matters is whether the ticket results in a conviction on your record, and how that record is treated over time.

No points does not mean no consequences. A conviction can still affect your driving record, your registration and your insurance, and license action depends on the record of convictions.

For how Driver and Vehicle Services handles records and license status, see the agency's site (Minnesota Department of Public Safety — Driver and Vehicle Services). Compare rules across states through the Governors Highway Safety Association (GHSA State Laws).

Does a no insurance ticket raise my insurance rates?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Minnesota-specific one, and your own change could be higher, lower or none.

A no insurance ticket is a little different from a typical moving violation, because it points to a gap in coverage. Insurers may look at the gap itself, not only the ticket. We do not have a sourced figure for that effect, so we do not give one.

  • Shop and compare quotes after you buy a new policy.
  • Ask any insurer how it treats a lapse in coverage and a conviction.
  • Keep proof of continuous coverage from now on.

We do not recommend any insurer. The only rate figure we use is the national ~20% average for moving-violation convictions.

Can SR-22, impound or registration problems follow?

Possibly. The national picture for this offense includes possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences on top of the fine. Whether any of them apply in Minnesota depends on the court, the citation and your record.

An SR-22 is a certificate of financial responsibility that an insurer files with the state on your behalf. If one is required, the state will tell you, and your insurer must be willing to file it.

  • Impound: a vehicle can be held in some situations. Ask the officer or the court what applies to you.
  • Registration: your vehicle's registration status can be affected. Check with Driver and Vehicle Services (Minnesota Department of Public Safety — Driver and Vehicle Services).
  • SR-22: confirm in writing whether one is required before you buy a policy.

Can a no insurance ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes courts have room to adjust an outcome, but we cannot promise that for Minnesota. The fine is set by the court within the state's traffic code, so the court is where any reduction would be decided.

Practical steps people commonly take:

  1. Read the citation for the court date and response instructions.
  2. Gather proof of your insurance situation, such as a policy document or a coverage record, and ask the clerk how the court treats it.
  3. Ask the clerk what your options are, including whether you can contest the charge.
  4. Consider speaking to a licensed attorney if the stakes feel high. We do not recommend a specific attorney.

TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and this is not legal advice. Court rules and outcomes differ, so confirm every step with the court.

What happens if I don't pay a no insurance ticket?

Ignoring a ticket usually makes it worse. Unpaid or unanswered citations can lead to further court action and possible license or registration trouble. The details depend on the court and are not covered by a sourced figure here.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court before the deadline on the citation. Ask what is payable, whether the state surcharge is included, and whether the court offers any alternative arrangement.

  • Do not wait for a reminder to arrive.
  • Keep copies of the citation and any payment receipt.
  • Confirm your record is updated after you resolve the ticket.

Because the amount is set by the court on the citation, the court is the only reliable source for what you owe today.
