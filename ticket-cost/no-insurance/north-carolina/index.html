How much is a no insurance ticket in North Carolina?

Nationally, a no insurance ticket costs $100–$1,500+ by state, plus possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences. In North Carolina the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles).

That national range is a national figure, not a North Carolina price. We do not give an exact North Carolina dollar amount because the amount on your citation is what counts.

The total you pay is usually more than the fine. Uniform statewide court costs dwarf the fine on most tickets, so the number on the court notice can look much larger than the fine itself.

Look at your citation and any court notice. The amount the court sets there is the figure that applies to you, and it can differ by county and court.

How is the fine for no insurance set in North Carolina?

The fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The North Carolina statutes are available through North Carolina statutes via Justia.

Because the court sets the amount, two drivers with the same charge can see different totals. The county, the court and the details of the citation all matter.

The main parts of what you owe are:

  • The fine: set by the court on the citation.
  • Court costs: uniform statewide costs that dwarf the fine on most tickets.
  • Other consequences: possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences.

For the most reliable total, read the citation and ask the court clerk what the full amount is. A clerk can explain the amount, but cannot give legal advice.

How many points does a no insurance ticket carry in North Carolina?

Our locked reference point system figures are for typical speeding: license points of 2–3, plus separate insurance (SDIP) points that can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier.

We do not have a locked point value for a no insurance conviction, so we will not state one. Check your citation, the court, or the North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles for how this charge is treated on your record.

It helps to know there are two separate systems:

  • License points: recorded by the state on your driving record.
  • Insurance points: used by insurers and can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier.

A charge can affect one without the other, so ask about both.

Does a no insurance ticket raise my insurance rates?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a North Carolina quote.

Whether a no insurance conviction is rated like a moving violation depends on the insurer and how it classifies the charge. Your own increase could be smaller or larger.

There is also a practical issue. A lapse in coverage can make it harder to find a policy, and an insurer may ask about the conviction when you apply. We do not recommend any insurer, so compare quotes yourself.

Insurance (SDIP) points can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier. If your charge carries insurance points, the cost over time can exceed the ticket itself.

What else can happen besides the fine?

The national range comes with other consequences: possible impound, SR-22 and registration consequences. These can cost more than the fine and affect your ability to drive.

  • Impound: a vehicle may be towed or held in some cases, which can add costs.
  • SR-22: a certificate of financial responsibility that proves you carry coverage. You may be required to file one after a violation.
  • Registration: your vehicle registration can be affected until coverage issues are resolved.

The rules and any fees tied to these steps come from the state. Check with the North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles for what applies to you.

For a broader comparison of state rules, the Governors Highway Safety Association — State Laws page is a useful starting point.

Can a no insurance ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes courts treat these cases differently, depending on the facts. For example, a driver who had valid coverage at the time may be able to show that to the court.

We cannot tell you whether your ticket will be dismissed or reduced, and we do not give legal advice. Outcomes depend on the county, the court, your record and the citation.

Steps drivers commonly take include:

  1. Read the citation for the charge, the court date and the amount.
  2. Gather documents that show your coverage status on the date of the ticket.
  3. Contact the court clerk to ask what options and deadlines apply.
  4. Consider talking to a licensed attorney if you have questions about your rights. We do not recommend a specific attorney.

Remember that court costs are added to any fine, so a reduced fine may still leave a sizable total.

What happens if I don't pay a no insurance ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can add consequences when a ticket goes unpaid or a court date is missed, and the state can take action on your license or registration.

We do not state specific penalty amounts or deadlines here, because the court sets them on the citation. The date and instructions printed on your ticket are what you should follow.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the clerk before the deadline. Some courts offer options, but that varies by county and court.

Do not assume the ticket will go away on its own. An unpaid no insurance ticket can add to the cost and delay fixing your record.
