How much is a failure to yield ticket in Arizona?

Nationally, failure to yield tickets fall in a range of $50–$500 by state (Arizona Department of Transportation — Motor Vehicle Division). In Arizona, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation.

That means there is no single statewide price we can quote. The number depends on the court handling your case and on what exactly you were charged with.

The base fine is only part of the bill. Court surcharges and fees are added by the justice or municipal court, and those amounts vary by court.

The fastest way to find your real cost is to read the citation itself and then check the payment or appearance instructions from the court listed on it.

How is the fine for failure to yield set in Arizona?

The fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. You can read the code itself through Arizona statutes via Justia.

In practice, the court named on your ticket determines the amount that appears there. Two drivers cited for the same type of violation in different courts may see different totals once surcharges and fees are included.

Several factors can change what is written on a citation:

  • The specific violation charged, such as a right-of-way violation at an intersection versus another yielding situation.
  • Whether the incident involved a crash, injury or property damage.
  • The court that has jurisdiction, whether justice court or municipal court.
  • Any surcharges and fees that court adds.

Because the amount is set on the citation, a number from a website or a friend's ticket is only a rough guide. Treat the citation as the authority for your case.

How many points does failure to yield carry in Arizona?

Arizona uses a points system for moving violations. The reference figure we have is 3 points for speeding. We do not have a verified point value for failure to yield, so we are not going to guess at one.

To confirm the points for your specific charge, check with the Arizona Department of Transportation — Motor Vehicle Division or ask the court clerk. The charge on your citation controls the answer.

The threshold matters more than any one ticket: 8+ points in 12 months means suspension or Traffic Survival School. A single yielding ticket may be well below that, but points from other violations in the same period add up.

If you already have points from other tickets, count them before deciding how to handle this one. The combined total within 12 months is what triggers the 8-point consequence.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance in Arizona?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not an Arizona-specific one, and individual results vary.

How your insurer treats a conviction depends on its own rating rules, your driving history and the details of the violation. A failure to yield involving a collision may be treated differently from one with no crash.

We do not recommend specific insurers or predict your premium. If you are worried about a rate change, ask your insurer how it rates a conviction of this type.

Keep in mind that the increase generally attaches to a conviction. How the case is resolved in court can therefore matter for insurance as well as for the fine.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but that depends on the court, the facts and the evidence. We cannot tell you whether your ticket qualifies, and nothing here is legal advice.

In general, people who receive a traffic citation have a few paths:

  • Pay the citation. This usually resolves the matter quickly, but it typically means accepting responsibility, which can bring points and an insurance effect.
  • Contest it. You can ask the court for a hearing and present your side. The court sets the process.
  • Ask the court what resolution options exist. Some courts offer alternatives. Availability varies by court, so ask the clerk or read the court's instructions.

Traffic Survival School appears in Arizona's point rules as a possible consequence when a driver reaches 8+ points in 12 months. Do not assume it is a way to clear a single ticket without confirming with the court.

If the stakes feel high, for example because you are near the point threshold, consider speaking with a licensed attorney. We do not recommend any particular attorney.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes the situation worse. Courts generally can add costs, and unresolved traffic matters can lead to license problems. The details depend on the court.

Because court surcharges and fees are added by the justice or municipal court and vary by court, delay can change the total you owe. Check the deadline printed on your citation.

If you cannot pay by the deadline or cannot appear, contact the court before the date passes. Clerks can explain what is possible, and acting early gives you more options than waiting.

Do not rely on a missed deadline being forgiven. Confirm any date, payment method and appearance requirement directly with the court on your citation.
