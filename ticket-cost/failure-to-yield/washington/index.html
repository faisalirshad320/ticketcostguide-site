How much is a failure to yield ticket in Washington?

The amount is printed on your citation or set by the court that handles it. This guide does not give a Washington dollar figure, because it would be a guess. Here is the lead figure we can source:

Nationally $50–$500 by state; in Washington the exact amount is set by the court on the citation — Failure to Yield Ticket cost in Washington. (Washington State Department of Licensing, last reviewed Oct 2026)

The $50–$500 range is national. It shows how widely states differ, and it is not a prediction for Washington.

Your citation and the court that issued it are the only reliable source for what you owe.

How is the fine set in Washington?

Washington sets the amount within the state's traffic code, and the court applies it to your citation. Traffic infractions are handled under the Infraction Rules for Courts of Limited Jurisdiction, known as IRLJ. The statutes are available through Washington statutes via Justia.

Statutory assessments are built into the IRLJ schedule amounts. That means the total on a scheduled infraction already includes those add-ons, rather than showing them as separate line items.

If you want the exact figure, read the citation, then check the issuing court's instructions or payment page.

  • Look for the amount due on the citation or in the court's notice.
  • Confirm which court issued it.
  • Ask the court clerk if the amount looks unclear. Clerks can explain payment steps but cannot give legal advice.

How many points does failure to yield add in Washington?

None. Washington has no point system. You will not see a number of points added to your record for this violation.

The state tracks driving history differently. Suspensions follow repeat moving-violation convictions within set windows. The practical risk is a pattern of convictions, not one ticket adding up to a point total.

For your own record and any suspension rules, the Washington State Department of Licensing is the primary source.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums ~20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Washington-specific or insurer-specific one.

Your actual change depends on your insurer, your driving record, and whether the ticket ends in a conviction. Some insurers look at the conviction on your record, not the citation itself.

  • Ask your insurer how it treats a moving-violation conviction before you decide how to respond.
  • Compare the fine with a possible premium increase over time, because the cost of a higher premium can exceed the fine itself.
  • This site does not recommend any insurer.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, depending on the facts and the court. We cannot say how likely that is for your case, and we do not give legal advice.

Washington traffic infractions generally give you more than one way to respond. The usual choices are paying, contesting the infraction, or asking for a hearing to explain your side. Your citation lists the options and the instructions that apply to you.

Points to weigh before choosing:

  • Paying usually ends the matter quickly, but it generally counts as a conviction on your record.
  • Contesting takes time and may require attending a hearing.
  • If the facts are disputed, such as who had the right of way, a hearing is where that gets decided.

If you want advice on strategy, a licensed Washington attorney is the right person to ask. We do not recommend a specific one.

What happens if I don't pay or respond?

Ignoring a citation tends to make things worse. Courts can add penalties, send unpaid amounts to collections, or take action against your driving privilege. The exact steps depend on the court and the deadlines on your notice.

Read the deadlines on the citation carefully. Those dates are set by the court, and we do not list them here because they are not part of our sourced figures.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court clerk before the deadline. Many courts have payment options, but you have to ask.

Could this ticket affect my license in Washington?

One ticket on its own usually does not threaten your license. The concern is repeat convictions. Suspensions follow repeat moving-violation convictions within set windows.

Because Washington has no points, you cannot count up toward a threshold. Instead, the number and timing of your convictions matter. If you already have recent moving violations, a new conviction carries more risk.

Check your driving record with the Washington State Department of Licensing if you are unsure where you stand. For broader context on how states treat traffic violations, see the Governors Highway Safety Association — State Laws overview.
