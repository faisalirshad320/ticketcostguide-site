How much is a failure to yield ticket in Vermont?

The honest answer is that the exact figure depends on your citation. Nationally the range is $50–$500 by state. In Vermont the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Vermont Department of Motor Vehicles).

We do not list a single Vermont dollar amount because the fine falls within the state's traffic code and the court fills it in. Any exact number you see on a third-party site should be checked against your own ticket.

The $50–$500 range is a national spread across states. It is not a Vermont schedule, and your ticket may fall inside or outside it.

Look at the citation itself. It should show the violation charged, the amount due, and where to respond.

How is the fine set, and what do surcharges add?

Vermont sets the fine for this violation within its traffic code rather than through one flat nationwide number. The court named on your citation determines the amount that appears there. The statutes are available through Vermont statutes via Justia.

Statutory surcharges are included in the waiver amounts. A waiver amount is the figure you can pay to resolve the ticket without contesting it, when the citation offers that option. That means the total listed is meant to be the full payment, not a base fine with extra fees stacked on top.

Things that can change what the court puts on the ticket include:

  • The specific provision you were cited under.
  • Whether the stop involved a crash or another violation.
  • Whether a waiver amount is offered for your charge.

We cannot say how each of these affects the dollar figure, because the amount is set by the court.

How many points is a failure to yield in Vermont?

Vermont's points system is described in our locked facts as 2–8 points by mph over, with 10 points in 2 years leading to suspension. That scale is built around speed, so it does not map neatly onto a right-of-way violation.

We have not been given a specific point value for failure to yield, and we will not guess one. Whether points are assessed, and how many, should be confirmed with the Vermont Department of Motor Vehicles.

The threshold to know: 10 points in 2 years = suspension. If you already carry points, ask the DMV how a new conviction would add to your record.

Drivers with a clean record and drivers with prior points can face very different outcomes from the same ticket. Checking your own driving record is the practical first step.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Vermont-specific one.

Your own change could be higher, lower, or nothing, depending on your insurer, your history, and whether the ticket ends in a conviction. We do not recommend any insurer, and we cannot predict your quote.

Points to keep in mind:

  • The ~20% figure is an average across the country.
  • A conviction is generally what matters to rating, which is why how the ticket is resolved matters.
  • Failure to yield tied to a crash may be treated differently by insurers than a ticket on its own.

If you are weighing options, ask your insurer how it treats a moving violation on a record before deciding how to respond.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but it depends on the facts and the court. We cannot give legal advice or say what outcome you should expect. What we can describe are the general paths a citation offers.

  1. Pay the waiver amount. Statutory surcharges are included in the waiver amounts. Paying generally resolves the ticket, which may mean accepting a conviction.
  2. Contest the ticket. You respond to the court named on the citation and present your side. The court then decides.
  3. Ask about other resolutions. Some courts offer alternatives. Whether Vermont does for this charge is something to ask the court clerk.

Failure to yield cases often turn on who had the right of way, which is a factual question. Photos, diagrams, and witness details can matter.

If the stakes are high, such as a crash, an injury, or points near the suspension threshold, consider speaking with a licensed attorney. TicketCostGuide does not recommend specific attorneys.

What happens if I don't pay or respond?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. The locked facts we use do not give Vermont's specific penalties for missed deadlines, so we will not state them.

In general, courts and motor vehicle agencies can add consequences when a ticket goes unanswered. Those can include added costs, a hold on your license or registration, or further enforcement. Check your citation and the Vermont Department of Motor Vehicles for the rules that apply to you.

Steps to take right away:

  • Read the response instructions on the citation.
  • Note any deadline printed on it.
  • Contact the court clerk if anything is unclear.

Responding on time keeps all your options open, including contesting the ticket.
