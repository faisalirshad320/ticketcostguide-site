How much is a failure to yield ticket in Nevada?

Nationally, failure to yield tickets cost $50–$500 depending on the state. In Nevada, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Nevada Department of Motor Vehicles).

We do not list a single Nevada dollar figure, because the amount depends on the court and the facts written on your ticket. Any specific number you see elsewhere that is not tied to your citation should be treated with caution.

The national $50–$500 range is a comparison across states. It is not a Nevada fine schedule and it is not a prediction for your ticket.

The number that matters is the one printed on your citation or shown by the court that handles it. If the citation does not list an amount, the court clerk can tell you what is owed for the charge as written.

How is the fine set, and what else gets added?

The exact fine for this violation in Nevada is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The code itself is published online (Nevada statutes via Justia), and it is the starting point for how a traffic charge is defined.

On top of the fine, administrative fees are added by the court. Those amounts vary by jurisdiction, so two drivers with the same charge can owe different totals if their cases are in different courts.

When you compare what you owe, look at the total due rather than only the base fine. Things worth checking on your paperwork:

  • The charge as written (for example, which right-of-way situation the officer described).
  • The fine line and any separate fee lines.
  • The court name and the date by which you must respond.
  • Whether the court accepts payment online, by mail or in person.

Failure to yield covers several situations, such as intersections, turns and pedestrians. The charge wording on your citation is what the court works from.

How many points does a failure to yield ticket carry in Nevada?

The Nevada context we are working from describes a points system of 1–5 demerit points by mph over. It also says that 12 points in 12 months leads to a 6-month suspension.

That scale is described in terms of speed. Failure to yield is not a speed offense, so we do not assign a specific point value to this charge. Confirm the points for your exact charge with the Nevada DMV (Nevada Department of Motor Vehicles) or the court handling your case.

Rule to remember: reaching 12 points in 12 months means a 6-month suspension. Points from separate tickets in that window add together.

If you already have points on your record, ask the DMV how many are current and when they fall out of the 12-month window. That tells you how much room you have before the suspension threshold.

A state-by-state comparison of traffic laws is also available from the Governors Highway Safety Association (GHSA State Laws), though it is not a substitute for your own citation.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance in Nevada?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Nevada quote.

Your actual change depends on your insurer, your driving record, and whether the ticket ends in a conviction. We do not recommend any insurer and cannot predict your premium.

Practical steps that do not require guessing:

  • Read your policy or call your insurer's customer line and ask how a moving violation is treated at renewal.
  • Ask whether the outcome of your case (conviction or not) changes how it is reported.
  • Compare the possible premium change against the cost of the ticket itself before deciding how to respond.

If the ticket involved a crash, the insurance picture can be different from a ticket with no collision. Your insurer or claims adjuster can explain how that works under your policy.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced in Nevada?

Sometimes a court outcome differs from the original charge, but we cannot say what is available for your case. Options and eligibility are decided by the court and depend on the charge, your record and local practice.

Generally, a driver with a citation can consider these paths:

  1. Pay the ticket. This usually resolves the case, but it can count as a conviction, which matters for points and insurance.
  2. Contest the ticket. You ask the court for a hearing and present your side. The court decides.
  3. Ask the court about alternatives. The clerk can tell you what the court offers, if anything, and what the deadlines are.

The citation itself usually explains how to respond. Follow those instructions and keep copies of everything you submit.

What happens if I don't pay a failure to yield ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts typically treat a missed deadline as a separate problem, and administrative fees are added by the court, with amounts that vary by jurisdiction.

The exact consequences depend on the court and on Nevada law. Common results of an unresolved ticket across the US include added costs, a hold or action on a driver's license, and a harder time resolving the case later. Ask the court clerk what applies to your citation.

If you cannot pay by the deadline, contact the court before it passes. Clerks can often explain whether a payment arrangement or an extension is possible, but that is up to the court.

Also keep the suspension rule in mind. Points matter because 12 points in 12 months leads to a 6-month suspension, and an unresolved ticket can complicate your record.
