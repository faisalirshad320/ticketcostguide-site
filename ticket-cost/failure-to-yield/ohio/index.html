How much is a failure to yield ticket in Ohio?

There is no single Ohio price for this ticket. Nationally the range is $50–$500 by state, and in Ohio the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Ohio Bureau of Motor Vehicles).

That means the number printed on your citation, or the number the clerk gives you after the court looks up your case, is the one that counts. A national range is useful for context, but it is not a quote for your ticket.

We do not state an exact Ohio dollar figure on this page because the amount is decided by the court that handles your case. Anyone who quotes you one flat Ohio fine without seeing your citation is guessing.

The $50–$500 range is a national figure that varies by state. It is not the Ohio fine for your citation.

How is the fine set, and what are court costs?

The fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The Ohio Revised Code is publicly available through Ohio statutes via Justia, and the right-of-way and yielding rules are found in its traffic chapters.

Fines are only part of the bill. Municipal court costs vary by court and often exceed the fine itself. Two drivers cited for the same yielding mistake in different courts can therefore pay different totals.

When you look at your citation or the court's payment notice, check for these items:

  • The fine amount for the violation.
  • Court costs added by the court that hears the case.
  • Any other line items the court lists separately.
  • Whether the court gives a total due, or only the fine.

Ask the clerk for the total amount due before you pay or plead, so you are comparing the full cost and not just the fine line.

How many points does failure to yield add in Ohio?

The point values we can cite from our locked data are for speeding. Ohio's system assigns 2 points for most speeding convictions, with 0 points for small overages in some circumstances and 4 points at 30+ over. Those numbers are not the failure to yield value.

We are not stating a point figure for failure to yield, because it is not in our verified data. The Ohio Bureau of Motor Vehicles is the place to confirm how many points a specific conviction adds to your record.

What does matter is the suspension threshold: 12 points in 2 years = suspension. Every conviction that adds points moves you closer to it, so one yielding ticket matters more if you already have other points.

Check your driving record with the BMV before deciding how to handle the ticket. Your current point total changes how much a new conviction matters.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance in Ohio?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not an Ohio rate and not a promise about your policy.

Failure to yield is a moving violation, and a conviction usually appears on your driving record. Insurers use that record when they price a policy, usually at renewal or when you shop for a new one.

The effect on your own premium depends on your insurer, your record, and whether the yielding incident involved a crash. If a collision happened, the claim itself can affect your rates separately from the ticket.

TicketCostGuide does not recommend insurers or predict your premium. If rates matter to you, ask your insurer how a conviction would be treated before you decide how to resolve the ticket.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but the outcome depends on the facts and on the court. Yielding cases often turn on who had the right of way, what the officer saw, and whether the intersection, signs or signals were clear.

Common paths people ask about include:

  • Pleading not guilty and contesting the citation at a hearing.
  • Asking the prosecutor or court whether a reduced charge is available.
  • Paying the ticket, which usually resolves the case as a conviction.

Which of these is open to you depends on the court that has your case. Court rules and local practice differ, so ask the clerk what the court allows.

This page is not legal advice and we do not recommend attorneys. If the ticket involved a crash, injuries, or a license at risk, you may want to speak with a licensed attorney in your area.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can add consequences when a ticket goes unanswered, and those consequences can include more cost and problems with your license.

Because municipal court costs vary by court and often exceed the fine, an unpaid ticket can become more expensive than the original amount. We do not list specific late fees or deadlines here, because they were not in our verified data and differ by court.

The citation or the court's notice should list how and when to respond. If you cannot find it, contact the clerk of the court named on your ticket. The Ohio Bureau of Motor Vehicles can tell you how unresolved matters may affect your driving privileges.

If you are unsure of a deadline, call the clerk before it passes. A short call is cheaper than a missed date.

Should I pay or contest a failure to yield ticket?

That depends on your situation, and we cannot make the choice for you. A few questions can help you think it through.

  • What is the total due, including court costs, and not only the fine?
  • How many points do you already have, and how close are you to 12 points in 2 years?
  • Do you believe the citation is wrong, or was another driver at fault?
  • Would a conviction affect your insurance, which typically rises about 20% on average nationally after a moving-violation conviction?

Paying is usually faster, but it usually means a conviction. Contesting takes more time and may mean a court appearance, yet it keeps the outcome open.

For rules on how states treat traffic offenses in general, the Governors Highway Safety Association state laws page is a useful reference.
