How much is a failure to yield ticket?

A failure to yield ticket carries a fine of $50–$500 by state, typically 3–4 points where point systems exist (Governors Highway Safety Association). That is the national spread. Where a specific ticket lands depends on the state statute, the court that handles it and the facts of the stop.

The range describes the base fine. Court costs, surcharges and administrative fees are set separately and vary by state, county and court. We do not publish an exact local figure, because the sourced data does not support one. The citation or the court clerk will list the amount owed in your case.

"Failure to yield" is a broad label. It can cover a driver who did not give the right of way at an intersection, ignored a yield sign, turned left across oncoming traffic, pulled out of a driveway into traffic, or did not stop for a pedestrian in a crosswalk. Some states write one statute for all of these. Others use separate code sections, and the penalty can differ between them.

Check these items on your citation before you decide what to do:

  • The statute or ordinance number listed for the violation
  • Whether an amount is printed or a court appearance is required
  • The payment or response deadline
  • Whether a crash or injury box is marked

The printed fine is rarely the whole cost. Insurance changes and court fees can add to it, and both vary by state and insurer.

Why does the fine differ so much from state to state?

Each state sets its own penalties for right-of-way violations in its vehicle code. Some legislatures set a fixed fine or a maximum. Others leave the amount to a court schedule. That is why the sourced range is wide, from $50 to $500, and why two drivers with the same mistake can pay very different amounts.

Several factors move a ticket within a state's range. None of them has a national dollar figure, so treat each as "varies by state."

  • The specific statute. Failing to yield to a pedestrian, to an emergency vehicle or at a stop sign may be written as different offenses.
  • Whether a crash happened. Failure-to-yield citations commonly follow crashes, and a crash can change how the case is handled.
  • Whether someone was hurt. Enhanced penalties apply in many states when the violation injures a pedestrian, cyclist or emergency responder.
  • The court. Counties and municipal courts often keep their own schedules and fees.
  • Your driving record. Prior violations can affect what a prosecutor or judge is willing to offer.

If you hold a commercial license, a violation can carry extra consequences. The rules for that vary by state and are not covered by the figures we publish.

The best way to find the real number for your ticket is to read the citation, then look up your state's vehicle code. State codes are available through State codes via Justia, though the court's own schedule is the final word on what you owe.

How many points is a failure to yield ticket?

Where a state uses a point system, a failure to yield conviction typically adds 3–4 points (Governors Highway Safety Association). That is the locked range we use. Some states do not use points at all, and in those states the violation is simply recorded on your driving history.

Points matter for reasons beyond the fine:

  • License consequences. Most point states set a threshold at which a license can be suspended or a driver must take a course. That threshold varies by state.
  • Record length. How long points stay on a record, and how they age off, varies by state.
  • Different systems. A state's DMV points and an insurer's internal surcharge points are separate things. A violation can raise one without the other.

Points are generally tied to a conviction, not to the citation alone. If a ticket is dismissed, or reduced to an offense that carries no points, the point total may change. Whether that option exists depends on the state, the court and your record.

Points can also stack with other violations from the same stop. A driver cited for failure to yield after a crash may also face other charges, each with its own consequences.

To see your own point total, request your driving record from your state's motor vehicle agency. Do not rely on a general estimate, because counting rules differ by state.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise your insurance?

It can, and the insurance effect is often larger than the fine. Failure-to-yield citations commonly follow crashes, which multiplies the insurance impact beyond the fine. A moving violation with no crash is one thing for an insurer. A moving violation tied to a collision is usually weighed more heavily.

Several factors drive the outcome. We do not publish a percentage or a dollar surcharge, because those vary by insurer, state and driver profile.

  • Fault. A failure to yield citation after a crash can be treated as evidence that you were at fault. That can affect claims as well as premiums.
  • Conviction versus dismissal. Insurers generally look at what appears on your driving record, so the outcome in court matters.
  • Your insurer's rules. Each company decides how long a violation affects pricing and how heavily it counts. State rules on how insurers may use violations differ.
  • Timing. The change often shows up at renewal, not right after the ticket.

TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer or tell you which policy to buy. If you want to know how a violation will be treated, ask your insurer or agent directly and compare your options.

When you weigh whether to contest a ticket, count the likely insurance impact along with the fine. A modest fine can still come with a meaningful premium effect, especially if a crash was involved.

What if the failure to yield hurt someone?

Penalties can be higher. Enhanced penalties apply in many states when the violation injures a pedestrian, cyclist or emergency responder. The details depend on the state statute, so the amount and the type of charge vary by state.

In practice, a violation that causes injury may be handled more seriously than an ordinary citation. Depending on the state, that can mean a higher fine range, a required court appearance, or a more serious classification of the offense. We do not list those figures because they are not part of our sourced range.

Right-of-way cases involving vulnerable road users often include these situations:

  • A driver turns across a crosswalk and strikes a pedestrian
  • A driver pulls out in front of a cyclist
  • A driver does not yield to an emergency vehicle with lights and siren active

"Move over" laws are a related but separate matter. They require drivers to change lanes or slow down for stopped emergency or service vehicles. Violations are charged separately from failure to yield, so a single stop can produce more than one citation.

An injury case can also lead to civil claims outside traffic court. That is a legal question, and this site cannot advise on it. If someone was hurt, a licensed attorney in your state is the right person to ask about your situation.

If your citation notes an injury, do not assume the usual fine range applies. Read the statute listed on the ticket and confirm the penalty with the court.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes. Which options exist depends on the state, the court, the facts and your record. None of this is legal advice, and outcomes are never guaranteed.

Common paths drivers look into:

  • Contest the ticket. You plead not guilty and ask for a hearing. The prosecution must prove the violation. Right-of-way cases often turn on who arrived first, what each driver saw and whether signs or signals were visible.
  • Ask for a reduction. Some courts or prosecutors will agree to a lesser offense, which may carry a lower fine or fewer points. Availability varies widely.
  • Traffic school or diversion. Some states let eligible drivers complete a course so the violation is dismissed or kept off the record. Eligibility rules vary by state.
  • Deferred or supervised outcomes. Some courts hold the case open and dismiss it after a period without new violations.

Be careful about paying right away. In many places, paying the fine is treated as a guilty plea, which can add points and affect insurance. That is a reason to read the citation before you act.

Evidence matters in a contested case. Photos of the intersection, signage, witness names and dashcam video can help. If a crash occurred, the police report and any statements you gave will also matter.

If the stakes are high, such as an injury, a commercial license or a record close to a suspension threshold, consider talking to a licensed attorney. We do not recommend any specific attorney or firm.

What happens if I don't pay a failure to yield ticket?

Ignoring a ticket usually makes it worse. The consequences vary by state, but common outcomes include late fees, a hold or suspension on your license, and a warrant for failure to appear. Unpaid fines may also be sent to collections.

Every citation has a deadline to pay or respond, and it is printed on the ticket or in the court notice. We do not state a number of days, because that varies by state and court. Missing it can turn a modest fine into a bigger problem.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court clerk before the deadline. Many courts offer options, though availability varies:

  • Payment plans
  • Extensions of the due date
  • Community service in place of part of the fine
  • Hardship or ability-to-pay hearings

If you are out of state when you get the ticket, the home-state consequences can still follow you. Many states share violation information with each other, so an unpaid out-of-state ticket can affect your home license. How this works varies by state.

Keep proof of everything: a copy of the citation, your payment receipt or confirmation number, and the date you contacted the court. If a record is wrong later, those papers help fix it.

How we sourced these numbers

The lead figure, $50–$500 by state, typically 3–4 points where point systems exist, comes from state statutes and schedules and was last reviewed in October 2026. We cite the Governors Highway Safety Association state laws page as the primary reference for how state traffic laws differ.

We use only that range plus two additional facts: failure-to-yield citations commonly follow crashes, which multiplies the insurance impact beyond the fine, and enhanced penalties apply in many states when the violation injures a pedestrian, cyclist or emergency responder. "Move over" laws are charged separately. Everything else on this page is described as varying by state.

We leave out any exact county, city or court figure. Local schedules change and differ by court, and a made-up number would mislead you. Your citation and your court are the authoritative sources for your own case.

For background on how crashes and risky driving are tracked, we point readers to two federal and industry resources, NHTSA and IIHS. Both focus on speeding, not on failure to yield, so we do not take any failure-to-yield figures from them.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a failure to yield ticket?

The sourced range is $50–$500 by state, typically 3–4 points where point systems exist. The exact amount varies by state, county and court. Court costs and surcharges are separate and also vary. Your citation or the court clerk can tell you the amount owed for your specific case.

How many points is a failure to yield ticket?

Where a state uses a point system, a failure to yield typically adds 3–4 points. Some states do not use points at all. How long points last and what total triggers a suspension vary by state, so check your state's motor vehicle agency for your own record.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance rates?

It can. Failure-to-yield citations commonly follow crashes, which multiplies the insurance impact beyond the fine. How much your rates change varies by insurer and state. Ask your insurer how it treats violations, since we do not publish surcharge figures or recommend any company.

Is failure to yield a moving violation?

Yes, it is generally treated as a moving violation because it involves how a vehicle is operated on the road. That is why it can bring points where point systems exist and can show up on your driving record. Exact classification and consequences depend on the state statute.

Can I get a failure to yield ticket dismissed?

Sometimes. Options may include contesting the ticket, asking for a reduction, or completing traffic school or diversion where offered. Availability varies by state, court and driving record, and no outcome is guaranteed. A licensed attorney in your state can advise on your situation.

What if I failed to yield to a pedestrian or cyclist?

Enhanced penalties apply in many states when the violation injures a pedestrian, cyclist or emergency responder. The amount and type of charge vary by state. Check the statute on your citation and confirm the penalty with the court, especially if anyone was hurt.

Is a move over violation the same as failure to yield?

No. Move over laws are charged separately from failure to yield. A single incident can lead to both citations in some cases. Penalties for each vary by state, so read each statute listed on your citation and confirm amounts with the court.
