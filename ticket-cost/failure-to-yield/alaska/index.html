How much is a failure to yield ticket in Alaska?

Nationally, failure to yield tickets run $50–$500 by state. In Alaska, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Alaska Division of Motor Vehicles).

We do not list a single Alaska dollar figure here because the amount is not fixed in the sources we use. The number printed on your citation, or provided by the court, is the one that counts.

The national range is useful for context. It shows how widely states differ, and it should not be read as what Alaska charges.

Bottom line: $50–$500 is a national range by state. Your Alaska amount comes from the court and the citation.

How is the fine set for failure to yield?

In Alaska, the fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The traffic code sets the framework, and the court applies it to the specific citation.

That is why two drivers cited for failing to yield can see different amounts. The facts of the stop, the citation itself and the court's schedule all matter.

You can read the underlying law in the Alaska statutes (Alaska statutes via Justia). Statutes explain the offense and the framework. They may not show the amount you will be asked to pay.

  • Start with the citation you were handed.
  • Check the court named on it.
  • Compare any amount you are told against the citation itself.

Do work-zone or school-zone locations make it cost more?

Yes, they can. Work-zone and school-zone violations carry doubled fines under the schedule.

If your failure to yield happened in one of those zones, the amount on the citation may be higher than for the same violation elsewhere. Look at the location and any zone notation on the citation.

We do not have a locked dollar figure for the doubled amount in Alaska. Do not assume a number. Rely on the citation and the court.

Doubling applies to work-zone and school-zone violations. It does not apply to every failure to yield ticket.

How many points is a failure to yield in Alaska?

The points system in our sources runs from 2–6 demerit points by speed. Points are tied to how fast a driver was going, so a failure to yield citation without a speed element may not map neatly onto that scale.

We do not state a specific point value for failure to yield. Check how your citation is classified with the Alaska Division of Motor Vehicles.

The suspension threshold is 12 points in 12 months. Points from different violations add up, so a single ticket may matter more if you already have others on your record.

  • Points range: 2–6 demerit points by speed.
  • Suspension: 12 points in 12 months.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance in Alaska?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not an Alaska figure.

Your own change could be higher, lower or nil. It depends on your insurer, your driving record and how the violation is recorded. We do not recommend any insurer and cannot predict your rate.

The ticket amount is only part of the cost. A conviction that raises your premium can cost more over time than the fine itself.

If you want to know your own situation, ask your insurer how a moving-violation conviction would affect your policy.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Possibly, but we cannot tell you whether yours can be. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and does not give legal advice.

Generally, a driver cited for a traffic offense can pay the citation or contest it in court. Paying usually means accepting the violation, with the consequences that come with it. Contesting means presenting your case to the court.

Before you decide, consider these questions:

  • What exact amount does the citation show?
  • Does the location trigger a work-zone or school-zone surcharge?
  • How might points and your insurance be affected?
  • Do you already have other violations that count toward the 12-point threshold?

For advice on your own case, speak with a licensed Alaska attorney of your choosing.

What happens if I don't pay a failure to yield ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts and motor vehicle agencies generally have ways to follow up on unresolved tickets, which can include license consequences.

We do not have locked figures on late fees or deadlines for Alaska, so we do not state any. The citation and the court are the right places to check how and when you must respond.

If you cannot pay the amount, contact the court named on the citation before the deadline. Do not wait until after it has passed.

Read the response instructions on your citation as soon as you can. They tell you where and how to respond.
