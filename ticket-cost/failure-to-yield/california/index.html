How much is a failure to yield ticket in California?

The honest short answer is that it depends on the citation. Nationally the range is $50–$500 by state, and in California the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (California Department of Motor Vehicles).

We do not publish a single California dollar figure here, because the amount you owe is the one printed on your citation or shown by the court handling your case.

The $50–$500 range is a national figure. It is a rough guide to how states differ, not a prediction of what a California court will set.

The base fine is only part of the cost. Added charges can raise the total well above the base fine, as the next section explains.

How is the fine set, and why is the total so much higher?

California courts set the fine for this violation within the state's traffic code, which is published in the California statutes (California statutes via Justia). The citation, bail schedule or court notice shows the amount for your case.

On top of the base fine come penalty assessments, county fees and surcharges. Together they multiply the base fine roughly 4–5×.

That is why two people with the same citation can see different totals. County fees and court practices differ, and the amount owed is the court's figure.

  • Base fine: set by the court on the citation.
  • Penalty assessments, county fees, surcharges: roughly 4–5× multiplier on the base fine.
  • Where to look: your citation, the court notice, or the court's online payment page.

If the number on a payment page looks high compared with a figure you read elsewhere, check whether it includes the assessments and fees.

What does a failure to yield citation mean?

A failure to yield citation says an officer believes you did not give way to another driver, a pedestrian or a cyclist when the law required it. Typical settings are intersections, left turns, merges and crosswalks.

The citation names the section of the California traffic code you are accused of violating. That section, and whether a collision was involved, can affect how the court handles the case.

Read the citation closely. It shows the violation, the date, the court and the instructions for responding. Those details decide your options and your deadline.

How many points does a failure to yield ticket add in California?

The point figures we can verify from our sources are for speeding: 1 point for ordinary speeding, and 2 points at 100+ mph. We use them only as a reference for how the system scales.

We do not state a point value for failure to yield. Your citation, the court and your DMV record will show whether points are assigned after a conviction (California Department of Motor Vehicles).

The threshold that matters most is this: 4 points in 12 months means suspension under the Negligent Operator Treatment System.

  • Check your own record before deciding how to respond.
  • Count any other convictions within the same 12 months.
  • Ask the DMV how a conviction would be recorded.

If you already have points from other violations, a new conviction matters more than it would on a clean record.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance rates?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a California-specific one.

Your own change depends on your insurer, your driving history, whether anyone was hurt or a collision occurred, and how the violation is recorded. We cannot predict your premium, and we do not recommend any insurer.

The insurance effect can outweigh the fine over time. When weighing options, think about the total cost, not just the amount on the citation.

A roughly 20% average increase is a national benchmark. Ask your insurer how a conviction would affect your policy.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but the outcome depends on the facts and the court. Your usual options are to pay, to contest the citation, or to ask the court about alternatives it offers.

  • Pay the fine: this usually resolves the case, but a conviction can add points and affect insurance.
  • Contest it: you can ask to be heard. Evidence such as photos, diagrams, witness statements and the officer's notes can matter.
  • Ask about alternatives: some courts offer options such as traffic school or payment plans. Availability depends on the court and on your record, so ask the clerk.

Because penalty assessments and fees multiply the base fine roughly 4–5×, a reduction in the base fine can change the total owed.

Deadlines are printed on the citation. Missing them can make things worse, so respond on time.

What happens if I don't pay a failure to yield ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes the problem bigger. Courts can add penalties and report non-response to the DMV, and your driving privileges can be affected.

The details depend on the court and on your situation. The citation and the court's notices explain what happens if you do not respond or pay by the stated date.

If you cannot afford the total, contact the court clerk before the deadline. Ask what payment or hardship options exist. Doing so early is usually better than waiting for a notice of default.

For license status questions, the California Department of Motor Vehicles is the authority.
