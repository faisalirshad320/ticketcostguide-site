How much is a failure to yield ticket in Pennsylvania?

Nationally, a failure to yield ticket runs $50–$500 by state. In Pennsylvania, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services).

We do not publish a single Pennsylvania dollar figure here, because the sourced material does not give one. The number printed on your citation or court notice is the one that counts.

The national range is a national range. It is not a Pennsylvania minimum or maximum, so do not treat $50 or $500 as what you will owe.

The fine is only part of the bill. Court costs, the EMS surcharge and other statutory fees typically exceed the base fine, which is covered below.

How is the fine set for failure to yield?

In Pennsylvania, the fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. That means the figure depends on how the citation was written and which court handles it.

The full text of the state's laws is available through Pennsylvania statutes via Justia. Reading the code can show you how the offense is defined, but the amount you owe will appear on your citation or court paperwork.

Things that commonly matter in how a court treats a yield citation include:

  • The specific section cited on the ticket
  • Whether the stop involved a crash or only a traffic stop
  • Your driving record
  • Whether you plead, pay or contest the charge

We cannot predict how any one court will treat these factors. Where the exact amount matters, the court clerk named on your citation is the best source.

Why is the total more than the fine?

Court costs, the EMS surcharge and other statutory fees typically exceed the base fine in Pennsylvania. This is why a ticket with a modest fine can still produce a much larger bill.

When you read your citation or payment notice, look for separate lines. Common items include:

  • The base fine set by the court
  • Court costs
  • The EMS surcharge
  • Other statutory fees

Because the fees are added to the fine, the total owed can differ from court to court. Always compare the total on the notice, not only the fine line.

We do not list dollar amounts for these fees, because the sourced facts do not give them. Ask the court for an itemized total if your notice shows only one number.

How many points does a failure to yield ticket carry in Pennsylvania?

The Pennsylvania points system, as summarized in our sourced facts, assigns 2–5 points for 11+ mph over the limit. A total of 6 points triggers a departmental exam/hearing. High-speed convictions add a 15-day suspension at 31+ over (PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services).

Those tiers describe speeding. They are not a point value for failure to yield, and we are not stating one here. Check your citation, your court notice or PennDOT for the number that applies to your conviction.

The 6-point threshold is about your total points. A yield conviction may add to points you already have, so your full record matters.

Points generally follow a conviction, not just a citation. Paying the ticket usually counts as a guilty plea, so think about the record before you pay.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Pennsylvania one.

Your own change could be higher, lower or none at all. It depends on your insurer's rules, your record and whether the ticket involved a crash.

TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer or give insurance advice. If you are worried about your rate, your insurer or a licensed agent can explain how they treat a moving-violation conviction.

Over several years, a premium increase can cost more than the fine itself. Weigh that when deciding whether to pay or contest.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but the outcome is up to the court. The citation will explain your options, which normally include paying, pleading not guilty and asking for a hearing.

Before you decide, it helps to do the following:

  1. Read the citation for the section cited and the court's name.
  2. Note any deadline printed on the notice.
  3. Look up the cited offense in Pennsylvania statutes via Justia.
  4. Gather any photos, diagrams or witness details from the scene.
  5. Ask the court clerk what plea and payment options apply.

If the stakes are high, such as a crash, a long record or a licensing concern, you may want to speak with a licensed attorney. We do not recommend any specific attorney.

We cannot say how likely a reduction is. We have no sourced data on dismissal or reduction rates.

What happens if I don't pay a failure to yield ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts and the state can add consequences for unpaid or unanswered tickets, and fees may increase.

The specific penalties and timing are set by the court and the state, and the sourced facts do not list them. Your citation and any follow-up notice should state what happens if you do not respond.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court clerk before the deadline. Many courts can explain payment or hearing options, though we cannot promise what yours will offer.

Keep copies of the citation, any notices and proof of payment. They help if there is a dispute about your record later.
