How much is a failure to yield ticket in North Carolina?

Nationally, failure to yield fines run $50–$500 by state. In North Carolina, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles).

We do not publish a single North Carolina dollar figure, because we cannot source one for every court and every set of facts. The national range is a reference point, not a quote.

The number that matters is the total on your own citation or on the court's payment notice. That total can include more than the fine.

Bottom line: the $50–$500 range is national. Your North Carolina amount is whatever the court sets on your citation.

How is the failure to yield fine set in North Carolina?

The fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The code itself is published online, and you can read the statutes through North Carolina statutes via Justia.

Several things can affect what appears on your citation:

  • The court that handles your case.
  • The specific charge written by the officer.
  • Whether the stop involved a crash, an injury or other violations.
  • Your driving record, in some situations.

Because the court sets the amount, two drivers with similar tickets can see different totals. Read the citation closely, since the charge listed there is the one the court will use.

Why can court costs be more than the fine?

Uniform statewide court costs dwarf the fine on most tickets in North Carolina. In practice, the fine is often the smaller part of the bill.

This matters when you compare numbers online. A page that lists only a fine may leave out the costs that every case carries. Your payment notice should show the full amount due.

If you are weighing whether to pay or contest, look at the total, not just the fine line. Any reduction to the fine may not change the court costs.

Tip: ask the clerk's office which amount is the fine and which are court costs. Do not rely on an estimate from a third-party site, including ours.

How many points does a failure to yield ticket add?

North Carolina uses two separate point systems, and people often mix them up.

The first is license points. For reference, typical speeding adds 2–3 license points. Our locked data does not include a point value for failure to yield, so we do not state one. The North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles is the place to confirm how your specific conviction is scored.

The second is insurance points, often called SDIP points. These are separate from license points and can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier.

  • License points are tracked by the state.
  • Insurance points affect what your insurer charges.
  • A single ticket can trigger both.

Ask the DMV or read your citation paperwork rather than assuming a number.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance in North Carolina?

It can. Nationally, moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a North Carolina quote.

North Carolina also has a separate insurance points system. Those SDIP points can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier. The size of any increase depends on your insurer, your record and how the conviction is scored.

Things worth knowing:

  • The increase usually follows a conviction, not just a citation.
  • Different insurers apply different surcharges.
  • The effect can last across several renewals.

Over time, the added premium can cost more than the ticket. We do not recommend any insurer, and you should ask your own insurer how a conviction would affect your policy.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but the outcome depends on the facts and the court. We cannot predict whether yours qualifies.

In general, a driver who receives a citation can pay it, which usually results in a conviction, or contest it in court. Courts may also offer other outcomes in some cases. Whether any of these apply to you is a question for the court clerk or a licensed attorney.

Before deciding, consider:

  • The total owed, including court costs.
  • The possible license points and insurance points.
  • Whether a crash or injury was involved.
  • Your own driving record.

What happens if I don't pay a failure to yield ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can add consequences when a ticket goes unresolved, and the DMV may become involved.

We do not list specific deadlines or penalty amounts, because we cannot source them in our locked data. Your citation shows your court date and instructions.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court clerk before the deadline on your paperwork. Courts often prefer that you ask than that you miss a date.

Do not assume a ticket will go away on its own. Read the citation and contact the court listed on it.
