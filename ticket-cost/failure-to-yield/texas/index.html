How much is a failure to yield ticket in Texas?

Nationally the range is $50–$500 by state. In Texas the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Texas Department of Public Safety).

That national range is a spread across all states. It is not a Texas fine schedule, and you should not read it as the amount you will owe.

We do not publish an exact Texas dollar figure for this violation. The amount is set by the court within the state's traffic code, and it depends on the court handling your case and the citation you were given.

The most reliable number is the one printed on or referenced by your citation. If it does not show an amount, the court clerk can tell you what is owed for that specific charge.

Treat $50–$500 as a national reference only. Your Texas amount comes from the court named on your citation.

What is included in the total I pay?

The fine is only part of what a court may ask you to pay. Court costs and surcharges are added on top.

In Texas, state court costs are uniform. Municipal add-ons vary by court. Two drivers cited for the same violation in different cities can therefore see different totals.

Before you pay, ask the clerk for the full amount due, not just the fine. Useful questions include:

  • What is the total including all court costs and add-ons?
  • Which payment methods and deadlines apply?
  • What options does this court offer for this charge?

Write down the answers and the date you asked. If you pay online, check that the total matches what the clerk told you.

What counts as failure to yield in Texas?

Failure to yield generally means not giving the right-of-way to another vehicle or a pedestrian when the law requires it. Common situations include left turns across oncoming traffic, entering a road from a driveway, merging, and intersections with stop or yield signs.

The offense is defined in the Texas traffic code. You can read the statutes through Texas statutes via Justia. Your citation lists the specific provision the officer relied on, and that provision is what the court will treat as the charge.

The exact wording matters. A citation for failing to yield at a stop sign is a different charge from one for failing to yield to a pedestrian, and the court may treat them differently.

If the incident involved a crash, the situation can be more complicated than a stand-alone citation. This page covers the ticket itself, not injury or property-damage claims.

How many points does a failure to yield ticket add in Texas?

None under a driver-point program. Texas abolished its driver-point and surcharge program in 2019. License action now follows specific offenses and repeat-conviction rules.

That means you will not see a point total rise for this ticket the way you might in a state that still uses points. It does not mean the conviction has no effect on your record.

Repeat convictions can matter. If you have other recent violations, check how they might combine before deciding how to respond. The Texas Department of Public Safety is the agency to ask about your driving record and license status.

For a comparison of how states handle points and violations, see the Governors Highway Safety Association state laws page.

No points does not mean no consequences. License action follows specific offenses and repeat-conviction rules.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance in Texas?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums ~20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Texas-specific one.

Your own result could be higher, lower or zero. It depends on your insurer, your driving history, and whether the ticket ends in a conviction on your record.

Texas having no driver-point program does not stop an insurer from using its own rating rules. Insurers look at violations and claims in their own way, and they do not need to follow a state point system.

If a crash was involved, a claim may affect your premium separately from the ticket. We do not recommend specific insurers. Your policy documents or your insurer can tell you how it treats moving violations at renewal.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but outcomes depend on the court, the facts and the charge. We cannot predict whether yours would be dismissed or reduced.

In general, a driver cited in Texas can pay the ticket, contest it in court, or ask the court what other options it offers. Paying usually resolves the case but may result in a conviction. A conviction is what typically matters for insurance.

Before choosing, consider:

  • Whether the citation lists the correct location, date and provision.
  • Whether this is your only recent violation.
  • What the court says about available options and deadlines.

TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and does not give legal advice. If the stakes feel high, such as a crash, a prior record or a commercial license, you may want to speak with a licensed Texas attorney of your own choosing.

What happens if I don't pay or respond?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts set deadlines, and missing them can lead to added costs or further action. The citation and the court clerk are the place to confirm your deadline.

License action in Texas follows specific offenses and repeat-conviction rules. An unresolved ticket can complicate your record, so it is better to deal with it than to wait.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court before the deadline and ask what it allows. Courts differ in what they offer, and the add-ons they charge vary by court.

For questions about your license or driving record, contact the Texas Department of Public Safety.
