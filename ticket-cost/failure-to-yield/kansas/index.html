How much is a failure to yield ticket in Kansas?

Nationally $50–$500 by state; in Kansas the exact amount is set by the court on the citation — Failure to Yield Ticket cost in Kansas (Kansas Department of Revenue — Division of Vehicles). The national range is a benchmark, not a Kansas price.

We do not quote a single Kansas dollar figure, because the court decides the amount within the state's traffic code. Court costs are added on top of the fine.

Start with your citation. It may list an amount, or it will tell you which court to contact to find out what is due.

Bottom line: expect the total to be the court's fine plus court costs. Confirm the figure with the court named on your ticket.

What counts as failure to yield?

Failure to yield generally means not giving the right-of-way to another vehicle, a pedestrian or a cyclist when the law requires it. Common situations include intersections, stop and yield signs, left turns across traffic, merging and entering a roadway from a driveway.

The officer writes the specific violation on the citation. The wording matters, because it determines which part of the traffic code applies and how the court treats the ticket.

If a crash was involved, the situation can be more serious than a plain citation. Other charges or civil claims may follow, and those are outside the scope of this guide.

Kansas traffic statutes are available through Kansas statutes via Justia. Reading the section cited on your ticket is the most reliable way to see what you were charged with.

How is the fine set in Kansas?

The exact fine for this violation in Kansas is set by the court within the state's traffic code. There is no single statewide number on the citation that applies everywhere.

Court costs are added by the municipal or district court. A ticket written inside a city is typically handled by that city's municipal court, while others go to a district court.

This is why two drivers cited for the same violation can owe different totals. The court, the exact charge and the added costs all affect the final amount.

  • Fine: set by the court on the citation.
  • Court costs: added by the municipal or district court.
  • National context: $50–$500 by state, which is not a Kansas figure.

For the total you would actually owe, ask the clerk of the court listed on your ticket. We cannot calculate it for you.

How many points does failure to yield carry in Kansas?

Kansas has no demerit-point system, so a failure to yield conviction does not add points to your license. That is different from states that assign a set number of points per violation.

Convictions still count. Three moving-violation convictions within 12 months can trigger suspension.

This means a single ticket may feel minor, but it matters if you already have other moving violations. Count any convictions from the past year before deciding how to handle the citation.

For your own driving record and license status, check with the Kansas Department of Revenue — Division of Vehicles. State traffic laws differ, and the Governors Highway Safety Association keeps a general reference to state laws.

No points does not mean no consequences. The conviction count within 12 months is what to watch in Kansas.

Does a failure to yield ticket raise insurance in Kansas?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums ~20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Kansas-specific one.

Your actual change depends on your insurer, your driving history and whether the ticket results in a conviction. Some insurers weigh a failure to yield more heavily if it involved a collision.

We do not recommend any insurer. If you want to know the effect on your own policy, ask your insurer or agent how a conviction would be treated at renewal.

  • The ~20% figure is an average, so your change could be higher or lower.
  • The increase is separate from the fine and court costs.
  • The effect on premiums can last beyond the date you pay the ticket.

When comparing options, weigh the one-time fine against the possible longer-term premium cost.

Can a failure to yield ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but there is no guarantee, and the options depend on the court. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and cannot tell you which path is right for you.

In general, you have three broad choices: pay the ticket, contest it, or ask the court about other resolutions. Paying is often treated as a conviction, so it may count toward the three-in-12-months threshold.

  1. Read the citation. Note the court, the charge and any deadline printed on it.
  2. Contact the court clerk. Ask what the amount due is and what options the court offers for this charge.
  3. Consider the record. If you already have recent moving violations, a conviction matters more.
  4. Get advice if needed. A licensed Kansas attorney can explain your situation. We do not recommend a specific attorney.

Ask whether the court offers any alternative resolution and what it would cost. Do not assume one is available.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can add penalties, and unresolved tickets can lead to further action, including consequences for your driving privileges. The details depend on the court.

Do not wait for a reminder. Look at the date and instructions on the citation, and contact the court if you are unsure what to do.

If you cannot pay the full amount, ask the clerk whether the court allows other arrangements. Policies vary by court, and we cannot say what your court allows.

If you plan to contest the ticket, do it through the court by the stated deadline. A missed deadline can limit your options.
