How much is a red light ticket in the US?

The national range for a red light ticket is $50–$500 depending on the state (Governors Highway Safety Association). That is a wide spread, and the figure on your notice depends on where it was issued and how.

The base fine is only part of the cost. Officer-issued tickets typically add court costs on top of the fine. Those costs vary by state, county and court, so we do not publish an exact local number.

If you want the precise amount for your ticket, look at the citation itself or the website of the court named on it. The citation lists what you owe if you pay without contesting it.

The $50–$500 range is a national spread, not a quote. Your own ticket could fall anywhere inside it, and court costs can change the total you actually pay.

Three things usually decide where you land in the range:

  • The state and its fine schedule.
  • Whether an officer or a camera issued the ticket.
  • Added costs, such as court costs on officer-issued tickets.

Other factors can matter too, such as whether a crash was involved or whether the driver has prior violations. Those rules vary by state, and we do not give figures for them.

How much does a red light ticket cost by state?

Each state sets its own fine schedule, which is why the national range is so broad. The Governors Highway Safety Association tracks state traffic safety laws, and state codes are published online through services such as state codes via Justia.

We list only the state-specific figures we can source. Everything else is described as varying by state.

  • Florida: the statutory red-light amount is $158 when paid as a camera notice.
  • Texas: banned new red-light camera programs in 2019.
  • Everywhere else: varies by state, within the national range of $50–$500.

Florida's $158 applies to the camera notice route. An officer-issued ticket is handled differently, and court costs are added on officer-issued tickets. Do not assume the camera figure is what you would pay if an officer stops you.

The Texas fact is about programs, not fines. It tells you that new camera enforcement programs cannot be started there, but it does not give a fine amount. For a Texas ticket, read the citation and contact the issuing court.

To check your own state, find the traffic code through the official state legislature site or a code library, then look for the red-light or traffic-control-signal provision. Fine schedules are often kept separately from the statute, so also check the court's published schedule if it has one.

Is a red light camera ticket different from one an officer issues?

Yes, in many states the two are treated quite differently. Camera tickets in many states are civil and carry no license points. Officer-issued tickets typically carry 2–4 points, and court costs are added.

A camera usually captures the vehicle and its license plate, not the driver. That is one reason many states handle camera notices as civil matters tied to the registered owner rather than as moving violations on a driver's record.

An officer-issued ticket is written to the driver. It is a traffic citation, and a conviction can be recorded on your driving record.

  • Camera notice: often civil, often no points, amount set by the state or local program.
  • Officer ticket: fine plus court costs, typically 2–4 points, possible record entry.

Because this varies by state, read the notice carefully. It should say whether it is a civil notice or a citation, and it should explain how to pay or contest.

Florida's $158 figure is for a camera notice. If an officer writes the ticket instead, expect a different process and added court costs.

Texas is a useful reminder that camera rules change. The state banned new red-light camera programs in 2019, so the rules in your area depend on local and state law.

How many points does a red light ticket add to your license?

Officer-issued red light tickets typically carry 2–4 points. Camera tickets in many states are civil and carry no license points at all.

The exact point value depends on your state's point system. Some states do not use points in the way others do, so treat 2–4 as a typical range, not a promise.

Points matter because they can add up. A driver who collects too many may face consequences under state rules, such as a warning, a required course or a suspension. The thresholds vary by state, and we do not state a specific number.

  • Check your state motor vehicle agency for its point schedule.
  • Check whether your ticket was a civil camera notice or a citation.
  • Ask the court whether a course or other option affects points.

Whether a ticket affects points can depend on whether you are convicted. Paying a ticket is often treated as an admission, so read the notice before you pay if points concern you.

We do not give legal advice. If points could put your license at risk, consider speaking to a licensed attorney in your state.

Does a red light ticket raise your insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, and your own change could be higher, lower or none at all.

The effect depends on several things we cannot predict for you:

  • Whether the ticket became a conviction on your record.
  • Whether it was an officer-issued ticket or a civil camera notice.
  • Your insurer's rules and your state's rules.
  • Your prior driving history.

Because camera tickets in many states are civil and carry no points, they may be less likely to show up as a moving violation. That varies by state and by insurer, so do not assume it.

A premium increase can cost more over time than the fine itself. The fine is paid once, while a higher premium can last for as long as the insurer considers the violation.

The 20% figure is a national average for moving-violation convictions. It is not a quote, and TicketCostGuide does not recommend any specific insurer.

To learn how your own rate may change, ask your insurer how it treats moving violations. You can also ask whether a dismissal or a civil camera notice would affect your policy.

Can a red light ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes. Options vary by state, county and court, and the right path depends on whether your ticket is a civil camera notice or an officer-issued citation.

Common routes people explore include:

  • Contesting the ticket. You can ask for a hearing and have the issuing party prove its case.
  • Checking the paperwork. Errors in the notice, such as wrong vehicle details, may matter. Rules vary.
  • Asking about a course or deferral. Some courts offer options that may affect points or the record. Availability varies by state.
  • Asking for a reduction. Some courts allow a request to reduce the charge or the amount. This varies by court.

For camera tickets, the owner-versus-driver question can matter. Because the camera usually photographs the plate, the notice goes to the registered owner. How a state handles that varies, and we do not state a rule.

Be careful about deadlines. Every notice has one, and missing it can remove your options. We do not give the length because it varies by state. Check the date printed on your notice.

Paying a ticket is often treated as an admission, so think before you pay if you want to contest. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and cannot recommend a specific attorney. If the stakes are high, a licensed attorney in your state can explain your options.

What happens if I don't pay a red light ticket?

Ignoring a ticket usually makes things worse. The consequences vary by state, county and court, and they differ for civil camera notices and officer-issued citations.

Possible outcomes include added late costs, collection activity, a hold on vehicle registration, or a license suspension. Which of these applies depends on where the ticket was issued. We do not give amounts or timelines, because those vary.

A civil camera notice and a citation are not enforced the same way. A citation is addressed to you as the driver and can lead to a court process if you do nothing. A civil camera notice is tied to the vehicle owner and may be enforced through other civil means.

  • Read the notice for the deadline and the options.
  • Contact the court or agency named on it if you are unsure.
  • Do not rely on a ticket being forgotten.

If you cannot pay the full amount, ask the court whether payment plans or other arrangements exist. This varies by court.

Ignoring a notice does not make the fine go away, and it can remove choices you would otherwise have. Acting early keeps more of your options open.
