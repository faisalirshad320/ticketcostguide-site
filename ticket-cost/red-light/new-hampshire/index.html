How much is a red light ticket in New Hampshire?

Nationally, red light fines run $50–$500 depending on the state. In New Hampshire the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles).

This guide does not give a single New Hampshire dollar figure, because the amount that applies to you is the one printed on your citation or confirmed by the court. Any website that quotes one flat number for the whole state is estimating.

The national range is a comparison across states. It is not a prediction of what you will owe in New Hampshire.

The base fine is not the whole bill. A 24% penalty assessment is added on top, which is covered below.

How is the fine set, and what is the 24% penalty assessment?

The fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The statutes are published online through Justia's New Hampshire code, and the court's number appears on your citation.

On top of the base fine, a 24% penalty assessment is added to every base fine by statute (Justia). It is a percentage, so a higher base fine means a higher assessment.

To estimate your total, take the base fine on the citation, work out 24% of it, and add the two together. Confirm the final amount with the court before paying, because the court's figure is the one that counts.

  • Base fine: set by the court on the citation.
  • Penalty assessment: 24% of the base fine, added by statute.
  • Total owed: the base fine plus the assessment, as confirmed by the court.

How many points does a red light ticket add in New Hampshire?

New Hampshire's points system assigns 3–4 points by offense. The number you receive depends on the specific offense on your record, so do not assume a number from a general article.

Points matter because they accumulate. Under the state's system, 12 points in 12 months can mean up to a 3-month suspension (New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles).

A single ticket will not reach that threshold alone. But points from other violations in the same 12-month period add together, and a red light conviction can move you closer to the limit.

To see what is on your record, check with the New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles.

Does a red light ticket raise my insurance?

It can. Nationally, moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a New Hampshire number and not a quote from any insurer.

What you actually pay depends on your insurer, your driving history, and how the violation is recorded. TicketCostGuide does not recommend insurers or predict individual rates.

The effect usually comes from a conviction on your record. How a dismissed, reduced or paid ticket is treated is a question for your insurer and the court.

  • Ask your insurer how it treats moving-violation convictions at renewal.
  • Ask the court how your case outcome will be recorded.
  • Compare the cost of the fine with a possible premium increase over time.

Can a red light ticket be dismissed or reduced?

You generally have a few choices: pay the ticket, or contest it with the court. Paying usually means accepting the violation. Contesting means asking the court to hear the case.

The court decides whether a ticket is dismissed or reduced, and the rules and procedures are set by the state. TicketCostGuide cannot say whether your ticket qualifies, and this page is not legal advice.

If you want help deciding, you can speak with a licensed attorney of your choosing. We do not recommend any specific attorney.

  • Read the citation for the court name, the violation and the response instructions.
  • Contact the court clerk with procedural questions.
  • Keep copies of everything you send or receive.

For background on how states handle traffic safety rules in general, the Governors Highway Safety Association's state law pages are a useful reference.

What happens if I don't pay a red light ticket?

Ignoring a ticket rarely makes it go away. Courts commonly treat unpaid or unanswered citations as a separate problem, and the consequences can include added costs or action on your license.

The specific penalties and timelines depend on the court and the state's rules. Look at the instructions on your citation, and contact the court if you are unsure what is required.

Because a 24% penalty assessment applies to the base fine by statute, the amount owed on a ticket is already higher than the base fine. Confirm with the court whether any further charges apply to a late or unanswered ticket.

If you cannot pay on time or have missed a deadline, contact the court clerk as soon as possible rather than waiting.

What should I check on my citation first?

Start with the details the court will use. Mistakes on a citation can matter, and you should know what you are responding to.

  1. Court and response instructions: who to contact and how.
  2. Violation listed: confirm it matches what happened.
  3. Base fine amount: the amount set by the court, before the 24% assessment.
  4. Total due: ask the court to confirm the total including the assessment.
  5. Points: check how many points the offense carries, from the 3–4 range, and how that fits with any others in the last 12 months.

With those details, you can decide whether to pay or contest the ticket, and weigh the fine against a possible insurance increase.
