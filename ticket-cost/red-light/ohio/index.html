How much is a red light ticket in Ohio?

Nationally $50–$500 depending on the state; in Ohio the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Ohio Bureau of Motor Vehicles). The national range is only a rough frame. It is not an Ohio fine schedule.

We do not publish an exact Ohio dollar amount, because the number that applies to you is printed on your citation or in the court's notice. Two drivers cited for the same violation in different Ohio courts can see different totals.

The figure that matters is the one on your own citation. Treat any single "Ohio red light fine" number you see online with caution, including ones from sites that do not name a court.

The total you owe is usually the fine plus court costs. The next sections explain how each piece works.

How is the fine for running a red light set in Ohio?

The exact fine for this violation in Ohio is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The code gives the framework, and the court that handles your case decides the amount on your citation.

You can read Ohio's traffic statutes through Ohio statutes via Justia. The Ohio Bureau of Motor Vehicles explains how convictions reach your driving record (Ohio Bureau of Motor Vehicles).

Practical points about how the amount gets set:

  • The citation or court notice lists the amount due or tells you how to find it.
  • The court that hears your case is the one that sets it, so the location of the ticket matters.
  • Whether you were stopped by an officer or received a notice in the mail can change the paperwork. Read the document closely to see which process applies.

Why can court costs be higher than the fine?

Municipal court costs vary by court and often exceed the fine. That means the fine alone can understate what you will actually pay.

When you look at your citation or the court's payment page, check whether the total includes costs. If only the fine is listed, ask the clerk's office what the full amount due is before you pay or decide to contest.

Compare the fine line with the total due line. The gap between them is where many drivers get surprised.

Because costs differ by court, we cannot give you a single Ohio figure for them. The court's clerk is the reliable source for the number that applies to you.

How many points does a red light ticket add in Ohio?

Our locked Ohio facts describe the speeding schedule: 2 points for most speeding convictions (0 points for small overages in some circumstances, 4 points at 30+ over). That schedule is for speeding. We are not claiming it applies to a red light conviction.

For a red light violation, confirm the point value with the Ohio Bureau of Motor Vehicles or in the statute text on Ohio statutes via Justia. Treatment can depend on how the ticket was issued, so read your citation.

The suspension threshold is a locked fact: 12 points in 2 years = suspension. Points from different violations add together, so one ticket matters more if you already have others on your record.

  • Check your current record with the BMV before deciding how to respond.
  • Remember the 2-year window when counting what is already on your record.

Does a red light ticket raise insurance in Ohio?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not an Ohio quote, and your own change could be higher, lower or zero.

Insurers set their own rules. Some look at convictions, some at points, and some weigh your claims history and how long you have been insured. TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer.

For a rough sense of exposure, take your current premium and consider what a roughly 20% increase would add over a year. Compare that with the fine and court costs when deciding how to handle the ticket.

The insurance effect usually follows a conviction on your record. How the case ends, and what your insurer sees, is worth understanding before you pay.

Your insurer or agent can tell you how your own policy treats a conviction.

Can a red light ticket be dismissed or reduced in Ohio?

Sometimes, but we cannot tell you whether it will happen in your case. Outcomes depend on the court, the evidence and the facts. This page is not legal advice.

In general, a driver who receives a citation can usually do one of these:

  • Pay it. This typically resolves the ticket but may result in a conviction on your record.
  • Contest it. You ask the court to hear the case. The citation should explain how to respond.
  • Contact the court. The clerk can explain what options the court offers and what the total cost would be.

If you want advice on your specific situation, you can consult a licensed Ohio attorney of your choosing. We do not recommend any particular attorney.

Read the response instructions on the citation first. They tell you how and where to respond.

What happens if I don't pay or respond?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse, not better. The court that issued the ticket decides what follows, and the notice you received should describe it.

Because we do not publish court-specific deadlines or added penalties, we are not stating any here. Look at the response instructions on your citation, or contact the clerk's office for the rules that apply.

Unpaid or unanswered tickets can create problems with your license. For how the BMV handles license status, see the Ohio Bureau of Motor Vehicles.

If you cannot pay the full amount, ask the court what it offers before the due date. Do not wait until after.

Points matter here too: with 12 points in 2 years = suspension, an unresolved ticket can leave your record unclear.
