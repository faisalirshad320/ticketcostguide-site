How much does driving on a suspended license cost in Minnesota?

Nationally, driving on a suspended license commonly costs $200–$2,500+, and it is a criminal offense with possible jail. In Minnesota the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Minnesota Department of Public Safety — Driver and Vehicle Services).

We do not list an exact Minnesota dollar figure on this page. We only publish amounts we can tie to a source, and the national range is a national figure, not a Minnesota price.

On top of the fine, a statutory state surcharge is added to every payable fine. That means the total you owe can be higher than the fine line on its own.

Your citation, and the court that handles it, are the most reliable places to find your actual amount.

Because this is a criminal offense and not a routine payable ticket, the dollar amount is only one part of the cost. Jail exposure, a criminal record and the effect on your driving privileges also matter.

How is the fine set for a suspended license charge?

In Minnesota, the fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. It is not a fixed number printed in a public schedule that applies the same way everywhere.

Several things can shape the outcome in a criminal case:

  • The charge written on the citation.
  • The reason your license was suspended in the first place.
  • Your driving and conviction record.
  • How the court resolves the case, including any plea.

Because the amount is set by the court, two drivers with similar citations may see different results in different courts. Treat any number you see online, including the national range here, as context and not as a quote.

You can read the state's traffic code through Minnesota statutes via Justia. We do not cite a specific section number here, so check the current text and the wording on your own citation.

The national range of $200–$2,500+ is a national figure. The Minnesota amount is set by the court on the citation.

How many points does driving on a suspended license add in Minnesota?

Minnesota has no point system. License action follows the record of convictions and specific high-speed offenses (Minnesota Department of Public Safety — Driver and Vehicle Services).

That does not make a conviction harmless. A conviction goes on your record, and the record is what the state looks at when it decides on license action.

If your license is already suspended, a new conviction for driving while suspended can complicate getting it back. Driver and Vehicle Services is the agency that handles license status, so it is the right place to confirm what you must do to be reinstated.

For a general view of how states handle driver licensing and traffic laws, the Governors Highway Safety Association state laws page is a useful reference. It is a comparison tool, not a source for your Minnesota fine.

Does a suspended license conviction raise insurance rates?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Minnesota-specific one.

Your own change could be smaller or larger. It depends on your insurer, your prior record and how the violation is reported and classified.

A suspended license can also affect insurance in ways that a percentage does not capture. Some insurers may review your policy, ask about the reason for the suspension, or change how they rate you. We cannot tell you how a particular company will act.

  • Ask your insurer how it treats a conviction like this before you decide how to resolve the case.
  • Keep your coverage active even while your license is suspended, if your insurer allows it.
  • Compare quotes only after you know what is on your record.

Can a driving on a suspended license charge be dismissed or reduced?

It can happen, but we cannot promise an outcome, and the rules depend on the court and the facts. Because this is a criminal offense with possible jail, it is worth treating seriously.

People in this situation often look at questions like these:

  • Was the suspension valid, and was notice properly given?
  • Had the underlying issue already been cleared before the stop?
  • Is there a record error with the licensing agency?
  • Does the court offer any alternative way to resolve the charge?

None of these is a guaranteed route. They are questions to raise with the court or with a licensed attorney who can advise you on your specific case. We do not recommend a specific attorney.

This page is general information, not legal advice. A criminal charge is a good reason to get advice that fits your situation.

If you plan to contest the charge, pay attention to the court date on the citation. Missing it can make the situation worse.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the citation?

Ignoring a criminal citation is risky. Because the offense carries possible jail, the court may treat a missed appearance seriously.

Common consequences of ignoring a citation in many places include added penalties, further license consequences and, in some cases, a warrant. We do not have a sourced Minnesota figure for any added penalty, so we do not state one.

Remember that a statutory state surcharge is added to every payable fine. If a fine goes unpaid, the balance you owe could still include that surcharge.

If you cannot pay in full, contact the court named on your citation before the due date. Ask what options exist, such as a payment arrangement. Courts differ on what they offer, so ask directly and get any agreement in writing.

What should I do after getting this citation?

A few practical steps apply no matter where your case ends up.

  1. Read the citation. Note the charge, the court, and any date to appear or respond.
  2. Check your license status. Driver and Vehicle Services can confirm whether your license is suspended and why (Minnesota Department of Public Safety — Driver and Vehicle Services).
  3. Do not drive until your status is clear. Driving again while suspended can lead to further charges.
  4. Contact the court. Ask how the amount is set and what a payable total would include, including the state surcharge.
  5. Consider legal advice. A licensed attorney can explain your options. We do not recommend a specific one.
  6. Call your insurer. Ask how a conviction may affect your policy.

The cost of a conviction is not just the fine. It includes the surcharge, possible jail exposure, the effect on your record and a possible insurance increase.
