How much does driving on a suspended license cost in North Carolina?

Nationally, driving on a suspended license commonly costs $200–$2,500+. It is a criminal offense with possible jail. In North Carolina, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles).

That means there is no single number we can give you. The range above is a national figure, and we do not present it as a North Carolina fine.

The amount printed on your citation, or announced by the court, is the number that applies to you. Nothing on this page replaces it.

The fine is only part of the bill. Court costs are added, and they can be larger than the fine itself. The sections below explain each piece.

How is the fine set, and what are the court costs?

In North Carolina, the fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. It is not a fixed price you can look up and pay online like a parking ticket. The court looks at the charge, and the result can differ from one court to the next.

Uniform statewide court costs dwarf the fine on most tickets. Those costs are added to the fine, so the total you owe is higher than the fine alone.

When you compare what you have been charged with what you read elsewhere, check whether a figure includes court costs. Many websites quote only the fine, which understates the real total.

  • The fine: set by the court on the citation.
  • Court costs: uniform statewide, added on top.
  • Possible jail: because this is a criminal offense, not only money.

Is driving on a suspended license a criminal offense?

Yes. It is a criminal offense with possible jail. That sets it apart from most traffic tickets, which are handled as civil infractions with only a fine.

A criminal charge can mean a court appearance, and a conviction can create a criminal record. The state's traffic code, available through North Carolina statutes via Justia, is where the offense and its penalties are written.

We do not quote a specific statute section or jail length here, because our sourced facts do not include one. Read the charge on your citation and the current statute text for the exact wording.

Possible jail does not mean jail is certain. What a court actually orders depends on the court and the case.

How many points does driving on a suspended license add?

Our sourced facts do not give a point value for this specific violation, so we will not guess. What we can say is how the system works.

There are two kinds of points. License points go on your driving record. For typical speeding, that is 2–3 license points.

Separate insurance points (SDIP) can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier. The two systems are different, and a violation can affect one without matching the other.

The 2–3 point figure is a speeding example, not a number for suspended license charges. To find out what applies to you, check your citation, ask the court clerk, or review your record through the North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles.

Does a suspended license conviction raise insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a North Carolina rate, and your actual change depends on your insurer and your record.

In North Carolina, insurance (SDIP) points can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier. Because that increase applies per tier, a more serious conviction can cost more than a minor one.

We do not know how any given insurer will treat a suspended license conviction. Only your insurer can tell you what it will charge, and we do not recommend any insurer.

  • National average: about 20% for moving-violation convictions.
  • SDIP points: can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier.
  • Your own rate: depends on your insurer.

Can it be dismissed or reduced?

Possibly, but that depends on the court and the facts of your case. We cannot predict an outcome, and nothing here is legal advice.

Because this is a criminal offense with possible jail, it is worth taking seriously before you plead or pay. Paying a charge usually means accepting the conviction and its consequences.

Practical steps that are open to anyone:

  1. Read the citation and note the court date and the listed charge.
  2. Ask the court clerk what your options are and what the total amount would be.
  3. Check the status of your license with the North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles.
  4. Consider speaking with a licensed attorney about your situation.

Whether a charge is reduced or dismissed is decided by the court, not by a guide like this one.

What happens if I don't pay or don't show up?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Because this is a criminal offense, missing a court date can bring additional consequences beyond the original amount.

Our sourced facts do not list specific late fees, deadlines or penalties for non-payment, so we will not state any. The court that issued the citation can tell you what applies.

Unpaid court costs and fines can also affect your driving record and your ability to get your license back. Check with the court and the North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles for your own status.

If you are unsure what you owe or when to appear, contact the court clerk listed on your citation before the date passes.
