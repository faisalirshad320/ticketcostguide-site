How much does driving on a suspended license cost in Alaska?

Nationally, a driving on a suspended license charge commonly runs $200–$2,500+. It is a criminal offense with possible jail, not a simple traffic infraction (Alaska Division of Motor Vehicles).

In Alaska, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation. We do not list a single Alaska dollar figure here because the number depends on the court and the facts of your case.

The $200–$2,500+ range is a national figure. Your Alaska citation, or the court that issued it, gives the amount that applies to you.

The fine is only one part of the cost. A criminal charge can also bring court costs, possible jail, and effects on your driving record and insurance, covered below.

How is the fine set in Alaska?

The exact fine for this violation in Alaska is set by the court within the state's traffic code. It is not a fixed number you can look up in a simple table.

That is why the national range is so wide. Courts can weigh the circumstances, and the same charge can end in very different outcomes.

Things that commonly affect the result include:

  • The court that hears the case
  • The details written on the citation
  • Whether the charge is handled as a criminal matter with possible jail
  • Any surcharges or costs the court adds

Alaska statutes are available through Justia (Alaska statutes via Justia). Reading the statute text is the best way to see how the offense is defined. We do not give legal advice, and a statute summary is not a substitute for the court's paperwork.

Are there extra court costs or doubled fines?

Under the state's schedule, work-zone and school-zone violations carry doubled fines. That rule applies to violations that occur in those zones.

We do not have a sourced Alaska figure for court costs on a suspended license charge, so we do not state one. Any costs added to your case will appear on the citation or in the court's notice.

If your stop happened in a work zone or school zone, ask the court whether the doubled-fine rule applies to your citation. Do not assume it does, because it depends on the violation charged.

How many points does it add in Alaska?

Alaska's points system assigns 2–6 demerit points by speed. Reaching 12 points in 12 months means a suspension.

That locked figure is described by speed, so it clearly covers speeding violations. We do not have a sourced point value for driving on a suspended license, and we will not guess one.

To find out how your charge is scored, check your driving record or ask the Alaska Division of Motor Vehicles (Alaska Division of Motor Vehicles).

If you are already suspended, new points and a new conviction can complicate getting your license back. Confirm your status with the DMV before you drive.

Does it raise insurance rates?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not an Alaska quote.

Your own change could be higher or lower. It depends on your insurer, your driving history, and how the conviction is recorded.

We do not recommend any insurer. If you are worried about your premium, ask your current insurer how a conviction on your record would be treated.

A suspended license can also affect whether you can get coverage at all. Your insurer is the only source for that answer.

Can it be dismissed or reduced?

Because the amount is set by the court on the citation, any change to the charge or fine also goes through the court. We cannot say whether a dismissal or reduction is likely in your case.

Common steps people take include:

  1. Read the citation closely for the court, the charge and any deadline.
  2. Check your license status with the Alaska Division of Motor Vehicles.
  3. Contact the court clerk to ask what options exist for your charge.
  4. Consider speaking with a licensed attorney, since this is a criminal offense with possible jail.

TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and does not recommend a specific attorney. Whether the charge was correct often turns on the details of the suspension, which is a question for the court and a qualified professional.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the citation?

Because this is a criminal offense with possible jail, ignoring the citation carries more risk than ignoring a routine ticket. We do not have sourced Alaska details on specific penalties for non-payment, so we do not list them.

The citation itself should say how and when to respond. Contact the court on the citation as soon as you can if you are unsure.

A further suspension is also possible under the points system, which sets a suspension at 12 points in 12 months. Whether your charge adds points is a question for the DMV.
