How Much Does Driving on a Suspended License Cost in Wisconsin?

By Editorial Team, TicketCostGuide · Last reviewed 2026-10-16

Nationally, driving on a suspended license commonly costs $200–$2,500+ and is a criminal offense with possible jail. In Wisconsin the exact amount is set by the court on the citation, so it varies by court, and a statutory surcharge stack is added to every forfeiture (Wisconsin Department of Transportation).

How much does driving on a suspended license cost in Wisconsin?

The lead figure we can source is this: nationally commonly $200–$2,500+; a criminal offense with possible jail; in Wisconsin the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Wisconsin Department of Transportation).

That range is national. It is not a Wisconsin fine schedule, and we do not publish a made-up Wisconsin dollar amount.

The statutory surcharge stack is added to every forfeiture. The total you owe is therefore higher than the base amount alone.

Your citation and any court notice are the only reliable source for your own amount. If the paperwork and a website disagree, follow the paperwork.

How is the fine set for driving on a suspended license in Wisconsin?

In Wisconsin the court sets the exact amount within the state's traffic code. The figure is written on the citation or provided by the court that handles your case.

This is why two drivers cited for the same violation can see different totals. The base amount, the surcharge stack and the court's handling all feed into what is owed.

The state's traffic statutes are available through Wisconsin statutes via Justia. Reading the statute that appears on your citation is the best way to see how your charge is classified.

The national description of this violation matters here. It is a criminal offense with possible jail, so it is treated more seriously than a routine parking or equipment ticket. A money penalty may be only part of the outcome.

Is driving on a suspended license a criminal offense?

The sourced national description says it is a criminal offense with possible jail. That is different from a basic traffic infraction that carries only a payment.

A criminal charge can mean a required court appearance. It can also mean a record that is treated differently from a simple forfeiture.

We cannot tell you how Wisconsin will classify your specific case. The citation lists the charge, and the court can explain what it means for you.

  • Check the charge wording on your citation.
  • Look for a court date and location.
  • Note whether the paperwork says you must appear in person.

How many points does it add in Wisconsin?

Wisconsin's points system lists 3 points (10 or less over), 4 points (11–19 over) and 6 points (20+ over). It also states that 12 points in 12 months = suspension.

Those tiers are written in speed-over terms. We do not have a sourced figure that assigns a specific number of points to a suspended-license conviction, so we do not guess one.

To find out what applies to your record, contact the Wisconsin Department of Transportation. It maintains driver records and can confirm your status.

Points matter here because the 12-in-12 threshold leads to a suspension. Driving while a suspension is in effect can add a new charge to an existing problem.

Does a suspended license conviction raise insurance in Wisconsin?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Wisconsin quote.

Your own change could be higher or lower. Insurers use their own rating rules, your history and the type of conviction.

We do not recommend insurers or tell you whether to switch. If you want to know how a conviction could affect your rate, ask your insurer or agent directly.

Treat 20% as a rough national benchmark only. It is not a prediction for your policy.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the citation?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts and the state can take further action on unpaid amounts or missed court dates, and that can affect your license.

The exact consequences are described in your citation and court notices. We do not state specific penalties because they vary by court and by the way the charge is classified.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court listed on the citation before the deadline. Courts can explain what payment arrangements, if any, they offer.

Since the surcharge stack is added to every forfeiture, ask for the total amount owed rather than only the base figure.
