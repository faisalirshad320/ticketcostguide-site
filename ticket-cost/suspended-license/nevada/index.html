How much is a driving on a suspended license ticket in Nevada?

Nationally, the cost is commonly $200–$2,500+, and it is treated as a criminal offense with possible jail (Nevada Department of Motor Vehicles). That is a national range, not a Nevada price list.

In Nevada, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation. We do not publish a single Nevada dollar figure because the court decides it within the state's traffic code.

Bottom line: the number on your citation or court notice, plus any administrative fees the court adds, is what you owe. Nothing on this page replaces that document.

The range is wide because the facts behind each case differ. A national ceiling of $2,500+ also tells you this is a much heavier charge than a typical parking or equipment ticket.

How is the fine set?

The court sets the fine for this violation within the state's traffic code. The amount is not a fixed number the officer picks at the roadside.

Because the offense is criminal and carries possible jail, it is handled through the court process. You usually cannot treat it like a ticket you simply mail in without reading the paperwork.

  • Read the citation for the charge, the court name and any listed appearance date.
  • Look for any amount the court lists, and ask the clerk whether administrative fees are included.
  • Check the Nevada statutes on Justia for the wording of the offense.

Amounts and fees vary by jurisdiction. Two courts in Nevada can add different administrative charges to the same type of citation.

What extra court costs and fees are added?

Administrative fees are added by the court, and the amounts vary by jurisdiction. We have no locked Nevada figure for these fees, so we do not give one.

That means the fine alone may understate what you pay. The total can include the fine plus the court's fees, and only the court can tell you the final number.

  • Ask the clerk for the total due, not only the fine.
  • Ask what payment methods and deadlines apply.
  • Keep a receipt or confirmation once you pay.

If you are comparing this with a speeding ticket, remember that the court-added costs follow the same rule: they depend on where the case is heard.

How many points does it add?

Nevada's demerit system is described as 1–5 demerit points by mph over, and 12 points in 12 months leads to a 6-month suspension (Governors Highway Safety Association). That description is built around speed.

We do not state a point value for driving on a suspended license, because none is in our locked facts. Ask the court or check with the Nevada DMV whether this conviction adds points to your record.

Why it matters: if your license is already suspended, extra points or a new conviction can affect when and how you can reinstate it. The DMV is the source for your record.

Also note that the point rule is a threshold. Reaching 12 points in 12 months triggers the 6-month suspension, so earlier points on your record count too.

Does it raise insurance rates?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Nevada quote.

Your actual change depends on your insurer, your record and how the conviction is reported. We do not recommend any insurer and cannot predict your premium.

  • Ask your insurer how a license-related conviction is treated under your policy.
  • Ask whether a lapse in coverage or a suspension affects your eligibility.
  • Compare the cost over several years, not only the first renewal.

A criminal charge with possible jail can matter to insurers beyond the average moving violation, so treat the 20% as a reference point and not a promise.

Can it be dismissed or reduced?

Only the court can dismiss or reduce a charge. This page cannot tell you whether that is likely in your case, and it is not legal advice.

Because this is a criminal offense with possible jail, many people speak to a licensed attorney before the court date. We do not recommend any specific attorney.

  • Gather your citation, any DMV notices and proof of reinstatement, if you have it.
  • Check the date and the details of your suspension on your DMV record.
  • Ask the court clerk what options exist for your case and what the deadlines are.

If a notice you never received led to the suspension, your DMV records and mailing address history may be relevant. Bring them to the court or to an attorney.

What happens if I don't pay or don't show up?

Because the offense is criminal and carries possible jail, ignoring the citation is risky. The court can treat a missed appearance or unpaid amount as a separate problem.

We cannot list the specific penalties for non-payment without a locked source, so confirm them with the court. Do not rely on a general guide for deadlines.

Practical step: contact the court named on your citation before the due date, and ask in writing if you need to confirm anything.

Unresolved court matters can also complicate getting your license back, since the DMV and court records are separate systems you may need to clear one at a time.
