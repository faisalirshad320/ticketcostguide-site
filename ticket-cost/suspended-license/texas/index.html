How much does driving on a suspended license cost in Texas?

Nationally, driving on a suspended license commonly costs $200–$2,500+. It is a criminal offense with possible jail, not a simple fine-only ticket. In Texas the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Texas Department of Public Safety).

We do not list an exact Texas dollar figure here. The national range is a guide to how wide the spread can be, not a quote for what a Texas court will order.

Your citation, court notice or the court clerk is the only reliable place to find the amount in your case.

The total you pay is usually more than the fine alone. State court costs are uniform, and municipal add-ons vary by court, so two people with similar citations can owe different totals depending on where the case is heard.

Costs beyond the court bill can matter too, such as reinstating a license and higher insurance. Those are covered below.

How is the fine set for a suspended license in Texas?

The court sets the fine within the state's traffic code. The amount is not a single flat number printed in a statewide chart that applies to everyone. What you owe depends on the court that handles your citation.

Several things can shape the outcome:

  • The court that hears the case and its local practices.
  • The facts written on the citation.
  • Whether the court treats this as a first matter or one involving repeat-conviction rules.
  • Municipal add-ons, which vary by court.

State court costs are uniform across Texas. Municipal add-ons are the part that can differ from one court to the next, which is why a neighboring city may charge a different total.

You can read the statutory language for yourself through Texas statutes via Justia. Statutes describe the offense and its penalties in general terms. They do not tell you what your particular court will order.

How many points does a suspended license add in Texas?

Texas abolished its driver-point and surcharge program in 2019. That means there is no point value to look up for this offense, and no point-based surcharge to expect.

License action now follows specific offenses and repeat-conviction rules. In practical terms, what happens to your driving privilege depends on the offense you were convicted of and your history of convictions, not on a running point total.

No points does not mean no consequences. This offense is criminal and can carry possible jail.

For current license status and reinstatement information, the Texas Department of Public Safety is the official source. A suspended license has a cause behind it, and what you need to do to clear it depends on that cause.

Does driving on a suspended license raise insurance in Texas?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Texas-specific one.

Your own change could be lower or higher. Insurers set rates using their own rules, your driving record and the type of conviction. We do not recommend any insurer, and we cannot predict what yours will do.

A few points are worth keeping in mind:

  • The increase usually shows up at renewal, once the conviction is on your record.
  • A criminal driving offense may be viewed differently from a minor ticket.
  • Some drivers who lose coverage need to shop for a new policy, which can cost more.

Because the increase is a percentage, the dollar impact depends on what you pay now. Ask your insurer directly how a conviction would affect your policy.

Can a suspended license charge be dismissed or reduced in Texas?

It is sometimes possible, but we cannot say whether it applies to you. Outcomes depend on the facts, the court and the prosecutor. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and does not give legal advice.

Common options people look into include:

  • Asking the court clerk what the citation requires and what the deadlines are.
  • Checking with the Texas Department of Public Safety whether the suspension was properly in place and what is needed to reinstate.
  • Speaking with a licensed Texas attorney about your situation, since this is a criminal matter.
  • Asking the court whether any payment arrangement is available.

Whether the charge is reduced, dismissed or resolved some other way is up to the court. Resolving the underlying suspension, where that is possible, is often a separate step from resolving the citation.

Do not assume a charge will be dropped because you have since reinstated your license. Ask the court how your case is treated.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the citation?

Ignoring a citation for a criminal offense is risky. Because this offense carries possible jail, the case is not just a bill that goes away if it is left unpaid.

The court's notice and the citation will say what you must do and by when. Read them closely and contact the court clerk if anything is unclear.

Missing a court requirement can lead to further consequences, and license action follows specific offenses and repeat-conviction rules in Texas. Additional problems with the court can also add to what you owe, especially since municipal add-ons vary by court.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court before the deadline rather than after it. Some courts have their own procedures, and the clerk can explain what your court allows.
