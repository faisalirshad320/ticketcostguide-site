How much does driving on a suspended license cost in Pennsylvania?

Nationally, the cost commonly runs $200–$2,500+. That is a national range, not a Pennsylvania price list (PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services).

In Pennsylvania, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation. We do not publish an exact Pennsylvania dollar figure, because the court decides it case by case and it can vary by court.

The fine is only part of the bill. Court costs, the EMS surcharge and other statutory fees typically exceed the base fine, so the total owed can be well above the fine line on the citation.

Read the citation and any court notice for the amount actually owed. That document, not a national range, is what counts.

Is driving on a suspended license a criminal offense?

Yes. Nationally, driving on a suspended license is treated as a criminal offense with possible jail, not just a fine-only traffic infraction.

That changes how you should treat the citation. A criminal matter can mean a court appearance, a record and consequences beyond money.

Pennsylvania's traffic code is available through Pennsylvania statutes via Justia. The text of the charge listed on your citation will tell you which provision you were cited under.

TicketCostGuide is not a law firm. We can show where the numbers come from, but we cannot say how your specific case will turn out.

How is the fine set in Pennsylvania?

The exact fine for this violation in Pennsylvania is set by the court within the state's traffic code. It is not a single number applied everywhere.

Because the court sets the amount on the citation, two drivers cited for the same offense can see different totals. The difference can come from the court, the facts of the case and the fees added.

  • Base fine: set by the court on the citation.
  • Court costs: statutory and typically exceed the base fine.
  • EMS surcharge and other fees: added by statute.

For the national context, the commonly cited range is $200–$2,500+. Treat that as a wide national guide only.

How many points does it add in Pennsylvania?

The point figures we can verify are tied to speeding. Pennsylvania assigns 2–5 points for 11+ mph over, and 6 points triggers a departmental exam/hearing.

High-speed convictions also add a 15-day suspension at 31+ over. Those rules apply to speeding. We do not state a point value for driving on a suspended license, because it is not in our locked sources.

To see how your own record is affected, check with PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services, which keeps driver records. Your citation and any PennDOT notice will show what was recorded.

If your license is already suspended, any further PennDOT action is handled separately from the court fine. Read every PennDOT notice you receive.

Does it raise insurance rates?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Pennsylvania quote.

Whether a suspended-license conviction affects your premium depends on your insurer's rules and your record. We do not recommend or compare insurers.

Insurers usually learn about convictions through your driving record. If your rate changes at renewal, ask your insurer what triggered the change.

On a $1,000 annual premium, a 20% rise would be $200 more per year, shown only as arithmetic on the national average. Your real change may be higher, lower or none.

Can it be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes a charge is changed or dropped, but we cannot predict that for your case. It depends on the facts, the court and the evidence.

Because the amount is set by the court on the citation, the court is where any change would be decided. The citation lists your options for responding, such as pleading or requesting a hearing.

  • Check the date and status of the suspension in the records at PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services.
  • Check that the charge on the citation matches what happened.
  • Consider consulting a licensed Pennsylvania attorney, since this can be a criminal matter with possible jail.

We do not give legal advice and do not recommend any specific attorney.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the citation?

Ignoring a citation for a criminal offense with possible jail is risky. The court can follow up on an unanswered or unpaid citation, and costs can grow.

The exact steps depend on the court. The citation and any later court notices explain deadlines and what happens if they are missed.

If you cannot pay in full, contact the court named on your citation before the deadline and ask what options exist. Court-set amounts include costs and surcharges that typically exceed the base fine, so ask for the full total.

Do not assume a missed deadline can be fixed later. Contact the court as early as possible.
