How much does driving on a suspended license cost in South Carolina?

Nationally, the cost is commonly $200–$2,500+, and it is a criminal offense with possible jail (South Carolina DMV). That range is a national one. It is not a South Carolina price list.

In South Carolina, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation. We do not publish an exact South Carolina dollar figure because the court determines it within the state's traffic code, and it can differ from one court to another.

Your citation and the court that handles it are the only reliable source for your exact amount. Statewide surcharges and court costs are added on top of the fine.

The base fine is only part of the bill. Read the sections below on surcharges, points, and insurance to see what else can add to the total.

How is the fine set for a suspended license charge?

Unlike many minor tickets, this charge is treated as a criminal offense. That means the amount is decided by a court rather than by a fixed fee that you can look up and pay online.

The court sets the amount on the citation, within the limits of the state's traffic code. The statutes are available through South Carolina statutes via Justia, though reading code language does not tell you what a particular judge will impose.

Several things can matter in practice:

  • The reason your license was suspended in the first place.
  • Whether you have prior suspended-license charges.
  • The court that hears the case and its local practices.
  • Whether other violations were written on the same stop.

We cannot say how these factors change the amount, because the locked sources do not give tiers. Treat them as questions to raise with the court clerk, not as predictions.

What court costs and surcharges are added?

Statewide assessments and surcharges are added to every fine and usually exceed it. For a criminal-level charge, that makes the total noticeably larger than the base fine alone.

We do not list individual assessment amounts, because we cannot confirm them from our sources. The practical step is to ask the court for the total due, not just the fine line on the citation.

When comparing what you may owe, compare totals including surcharges. A fine that looks small can still produce a large bill once assessments are added.

The court or its clerk can tell you the full amount and which payment methods they accept. Keep a written record of what you were told and when.

How many points does it add to my record?

The state points system we reference runs 2–6 points by mph over, and 12 points = suspension. Points halve after 12 months. That scale is written around speeding violations.

We do not state a specific point value for driving on a suspended license, because it is not part of the facts we have verified. Your citation, the court, or the South Carolina Department of Motor Vehicles can confirm how your conviction is recorded.

The more important point is that this offense happens while your license is already suspended. A conviction can complicate getting your driving privileges back, so ask the DMV what applies to your situation.

  • Points are tracked separately from the fine.
  • Reaching 12 points leads to suspension.
  • Points halve after 12 months under the locked state context.

Does it raise my insurance rates?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a South Carolina rate.

A suspended-license conviction can be viewed differently from an ordinary moving violation, and insurers set their own rules. We do not give a South Carolina or per-insurer number because we cannot source one.

What you can do is ask your insurer how it treats a conviction like this, and whether the suspension itself has already affected your policy. TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer.

The national ~20% average is a rough guide only. Your actual change depends on your insurer, your history, and the reason for the suspension.

Can the charge be dismissed or reduced?

We cannot tell you whether your charge can be dismissed or reduced. Because it is a criminal offense with possible jail, the stakes are higher than for a typical ticket, and outcomes depend on the facts and the court.

General options people look into include the following:

  • Reading the citation to confirm the charge, the court, and the date you must respond.
  • Contacting the court clerk to learn how the case is handled and what the total amount is.
  • Checking your driving record with the South Carolina DMV to confirm that your license was suspended on the date of the stop.
  • Speaking with a licensed attorney who can advise on your case. We do not recommend a specific attorney.

This is general information, not legal advice. Because jail is possible, many people choose to get qualified legal advice before they respond.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the citation?

We cannot give specific penalties for non-payment, because the locked sources do not list them. What we can say is that the charge is criminal and jail is possible, so ignoring the citation carries real risk.

Courts generally expect a response by the date on the citation. Missing it can lead to additional consequences, and the court can tell you what applies to your case.

If you cannot pay the total, contact the court clerk before the deadline and ask what options exist. Do not assume the amount will shrink or disappear with time.

Surcharges are added to every fine and usually exceed it, so ask for the full total, not only the fine.
