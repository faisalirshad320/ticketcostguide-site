How much does driving on a suspended license cost in Arizona?

Nationally, driving on a suspended license commonly costs $200–$2,500+. It is a criminal offense with possible jail. In Arizona, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Arizona Department of Transportation — Motor Vehicle Division).

We do not publish an exact Arizona dollar figure, because the amount depends on the court and the facts of your case. The national range is a general guide to how wide the spread can be.

The number that matters is the one on your citation or in the court's notice. Treat the national range as context only.

The fine is also only one piece of the cost. Court surcharges and fees are added on top, and those can vary by court.

How is the fine set in Arizona?

The exact fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. That means the judge or court sets the amount, not a single flat number printed in a statewide chart.

You can read the traffic code itself through Arizona statutes via Justia. The Motor Vehicle Division explains license status and reinstatement on its official site.

  • The citation: it shows the charge and the court that will handle it.
  • The court: a justice court or municipal court adds its own surcharges and fees.
  • Your record: courts may treat cases differently depending on the circumstances, so ask the clerk how your case is handled.

Because amounts vary by court, two drivers charged with the same offense can owe different totals.

What surcharges and court fees get added?

Court surcharges and fees are added by the justice or municipal court, and amounts vary by court. We do not list specific surcharge amounts because the sources we rely on do not fix one statewide figure.

To see your total, check the citation, the court's payment notice or the court's website. If the paperwork shows a base fine and a separate total, the difference is usually surcharges and fees.

Ask the clerk to confirm the full amount before you pay. That also helps you avoid paying the wrong court if more than one is involved.

How many points does driving on a suspended license carry in Arizona?

Our sources do not give a specific point value for this offense. For comparison, Arizona's system assigns 3 points for speeding.

The key threshold is 8+ points in 12 months, which leads to a suspension or Traffic Survival School. Points from other violations can add up toward that threshold.

To see how your own record stands, check with the Motor Vehicle Division. It keeps your driving record and can tell you which violations were counted.

A further suspension can also follow a conviction. Ask the court or the MVD what applies to your license status.

Is driving on a suspended license a crime in Arizona?

Nationally, driving on a suspended license is a criminal offense with possible jail. That makes it different from a typical civil traffic infraction such as a basic speeding ticket.

The specific penalty in Arizona depends on the charge on your citation and the court's decision. We do not state jail terms, because our sources do not provide a verified figure.

Because jail is possible, read the citation closely and attend every court date. If you have questions about your rights or the charge, a licensed attorney can give legal advice. TicketCostGuide cannot.

Does a suspended license conviction raise insurance in Arizona?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a quote for Arizona or for any one insurer.

Your actual change depends on your insurer, your record and how the violation is classified. A conviction for driving on a suspended license is a serious entry on a driving record, so ask your insurer how it would treat one.

  • Do not assume the 20% figure applies to you.
  • Ask your insurer directly how a conviction would affect your rate.
  • Check your record with the MVD so you know what insurers may see.

Can it be dismissed or reduced, and what if I don't pay?

Whether a charge can be dismissed or reduced is up to the court and the prosecutor. Our sources do not list a standard outcome, so we do not promise one.

What you can do is respond on time. Read the citation, note the court and the date, and contact the clerk with questions about payment, scheduling or options. An attorney can advise you on strategy.

If you ignore the citation, the problem usually grows. Missed deadlines can bring added court costs, further license consequences or other court action. Because this is a criminal offense with possible jail, ignoring it carries real risk.

If you cannot pay the full amount at once, ask the court clerk what payment arrangements exist. Do this before the deadline passes.
