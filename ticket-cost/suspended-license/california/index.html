How much does driving on a suspended license cost in California?

Nationally, the cost is commonly $200–$2,500+, and it is a criminal offense with possible jail. In California, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (California Department of Motor Vehicles).

We do not publish a single California dollar figure for this violation. The amount depends on the court and the facts of your case, and any number we gave would be a guess.

The national range is wide for a reason. Fines vary by state, and the amount in a given case can depend on the reason for the suspension and your driving record. Court costs and surcharges are added on top.

The $200–$2,500+ range is national. Your California citation and the court that issued it control what you actually owe.

Read the citation closely. It usually names the court, the code section charged and the date you must respond.

How is the fine set, and why is the total higher than the base fine?

In California, the court sets the exact fine for this violation within the state's traffic code. The statutes are available through California statutes via Justia.

The base fine is only part of the bill. Penalty assessments, county fees and surcharges multiply the base fine roughly 4–5×.

This is why people are often surprised by the final total. A court that sets a modest base fine can still produce a much larger amount after the add-ons.

  • Base fine: set by the court within the state's traffic code.
  • Penalty assessments: added on top of the base fine.
  • County fees and surcharges: vary by county and court.

Because these add-ons vary by county and court, two people charged with the same offense can owe different totals. Ask the court clerk for the full amount due, not just the base fine.

Since this is a criminal offense, the court may also impose penalties beyond money, and jail is possible. The citation or the court can tell you what applies to your case.

How many points does driving on a suspended license add in California?

Our locked sources give point values only for speeding: 1 point for ordinary speeding, and 2 points at 100+ mph. They do not give a point value for driving on a suspended license, so we do not state one.

Check your driving record or ask the California Department of Motor Vehicles how a conviction for this offense is counted.

The threshold that matters is 4 points in 12 months, which results in a suspension under the Negligent Operator Treatment System.

A suspended license is often the result of an earlier problem. A new conviction while suspended can add to that record rather than replace it.

Because the point total can affect whether your license stays suspended, review your record before you decide how to respond to the citation.

Does it raise insurance rates?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a California quote.

We have no locked figure for how much this specific offense changes a California premium. The effect depends on your insurer, your history and how the conviction is recorded.

Some practical points:

  • A conviction can show up when your insurer checks your driving record at renewal.
  • A suspended license may already have affected your coverage, depending on why it was suspended.
  • Rates vary by insurer, so the only reliable number is the one in your own quote.

TicketCostGuide does not recommend specific insurers. If you are worried about your premium, ask your own insurer how a conviction would be treated.

Can it be dismissed or reduced?

That is decided by the court and the prosecutor on the facts of your case. We cannot say whether it can happen for you, and this page is not legal advice.

Some general points are worth knowing. This is a criminal offense, so the process is more serious than for a standard infraction.

  • The citation names the court and the date you must respond.
  • The court clerk can explain what options exist and what documents the court needs.
  • The state's traffic code sets the range the court works within. You can read it at California statutes via Justia.

Because the offense can carry possible jail, many people choose to speak with a licensed attorney before they respond. We do not recommend a specific attorney. Your county bar association or the court's self-help resources are neutral places to start.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the citation?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. The court can add penalties, and the penalty assessments, county fees and surcharges that multiply the base fine roughly 4–5× still apply.

Because this is a criminal offense with possible jail, missing a court date is a serious step. Contact the court named on your citation as soon as you can.

Unpaid matters can also affect your driving privilege. Your license may already be suspended, so further problems on your record could make it harder to reinstate. Ask the California Department of Motor Vehicles what is required to reinstate your license.

If you cannot pay the full amount, ask the court clerk what payment options exist before the due date, not after.

What should I do after getting this citation?

Start with the paper in your hand. Find the court, the due date and the charge listed.

  1. Read the citation and note the court and the response date.
  2. Contact the court clerk and ask for the total amount due, including penalty assessments and fees.
  3. Check your license status and record with the California DMV.
  4. Consider speaking with a licensed attorney, given the possibility of jail.
  5. Do not drive until your license status is resolved.

State driving laws differ, and the Governors Highway Safety Association keeps a general overview at GHSA State Laws. It does not replace your court's instructions.
