How much does driving on a suspended license cost in New Hampshire?

Nationally, the cost is commonly $200–$2,500+, and it is a criminal offense with possible jail. In New Hampshire, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles).

That means there is no single sticker price we can honestly quote for every driver. The number on your paperwork depends on the court handling the case and the details of the charge.

The national range is a national figure. It is not a New Hampshire fine schedule. Read the amount on your own citation or court notice.

The fine is only part of the cost. A 24% penalty assessment is added, and a conviction can affect your record and your insurance. The sections below cover each piece.

How is the fine set for driving on a suspended license?

In New Hampshire, the fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The amount appears on the citation or in the court's notice to you.

Because this is treated as a criminal offense rather than a simple infraction, the court has more discretion than it does with a basic speeding ticket. Jail is a possibility, which is why this charge is taken more seriously than most moving violations.

  • The citation: This is the first place to look for the amount.
  • The court: It sets the fine within the state's traffic code, so it can vary from case to case.
  • The statute: The New Hampshire statutes are available online through Justia.

If the paperwork does not show an amount, contact the court clerk listed on the citation. Clerks can explain what is owed and how to pay, though they cannot give legal advice.

Is anything added on top of the base fine?

Yes. A 24% penalty assessment is added to every base fine by statute (Justia). That applies to this violation as it does to other fines.

In practice, your total will be higher than the base fine alone. When you read a figure on a citation, check whether it is the base fine or the total with the assessment included.

Ask the court clerk to confirm the total due, including the 24% penalty assessment, before you pay.

We do not list other fees here because we have not verified a dollar figure for them. Any additional costs, such as court-specific charges or reinstatement requirements, should be confirmed with the court and the Division of Motor Vehicles.

How many points does driving on a suspended license carry in New Hampshire?

New Hampshire's points system assigns 3–4 points by offense. Under that system, 12 points in 12 months means up to a 3-month suspension (New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles).

The exact point value attached to your specific charge is shown by the offense on your record. Because this violation is criminal and the court decides the outcome, check how your conviction is recorded rather than assuming a number.

  • Points range: 3–4 points by offense.
  • Threshold: 12 points in 12 months.
  • Consequence: up to a 3-month suspension.

Driving while your license is already suspended can make your situation worse. If you also pick up points from other tickets, you may be moving closer to a new suspension. The Division of Motor Vehicles can confirm your current point total.

Does a suspended license conviction raise insurance rates?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a New Hampshire-specific rate.

Insurers set their own prices, so your actual change could be higher, lower or nothing at all. It depends on your insurer, your driving history and how the conviction is recorded. We do not recommend any insurer, and we cannot predict your quote.

  • Check your policy for how it treats suspensions and convictions.
  • Ask your insurer directly how a conviction would affect renewal.
  • Compare the possible premium change against the fine when you weigh your options.

For many drivers, the long-term insurance cost is a bigger factor than the fine itself. That is worth keeping in mind before you decide how to respond.

Can a suspended license charge be dismissed or reduced?

It can happen in some cases, but outcomes depend on the facts and on the court. We cannot tell you whether yours qualifies, and nothing here is legal advice.

Because this is a criminal offense with possible jail, many people choose to talk with a licensed attorney before responding. We do not recommend a specific attorney. Your state or local bar association can point you toward referral services.

  • Read the citation: Confirm the charge, the court and any response instructions.
  • Check your license status: The Division of Motor Vehicles can show whether your suspension was active on the date of the stop.
  • Ask the court: The clerk can explain procedures and deadlines, though not advise on strategy.
  • Consider legal help: A lawyer can explain whether reduction or dismissal is realistic.

Do not ignore the citation. The next section explains why.

What happens if I don't pay or respond?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Because this is a criminal matter, the court may take further action if you do not respond as directed. The exact steps depend on the court and the citation.

An unresolved case can also complicate efforts to restore your license. A suspension that stays in place keeps you from driving legally, and driving again risks another charge.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court clerk before the due date to ask what options exist.

For the current status of your license, contact the New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles. For how other states handle similar laws, the Governors Highway Safety Association keeps a state laws overview.
