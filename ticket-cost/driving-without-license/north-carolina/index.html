How much does driving without a license cost in North Carolina?

Nationally, a first offense for driving without a license commonly runs $50–$500, and it is often chargeable as a misdemeanor (North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles). That is a national range, not a North Carolina schedule.

In North Carolina, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation. We do not publish a single state dollar figure because the court decides it within the state's traffic code.

Your citation and the court that hears it control the number you owe. Read the citation, then check with the clerk of court for the amount due.

The fine is only part of the bill. Court costs apply as well, as covered below.

How is the fine set?

The offense and its penalty range come from North Carolina's traffic code, which you can read through North Carolina statutes via Justia. Within that range, the court sets the amount that appears on your citation.

Several things can affect what the court does with a case:

  • Whether you never held a license or held one that was suspended, revoked or expired. These are often treated as different situations.
  • Whether this is a first offense or you have prior convictions.
  • Whether the charge is a misdemeanor, which is often the case nationally for this violation.
  • Whether other violations were written on the same citation.

Because the court sets the amount, two drivers charged with the same offense can see different totals. Treat any online number, including the national range here, as a guide only.

Why do court costs matter more than the fine?

North Carolina applies uniform statewide court costs. On most tickets, those costs dwarf the fine itself.

That means the amount you pay at the clerk's office can be well above the fine printed on the citation. Ask for the full amount due, not just the fine.

  • The fine is the court's penalty for the violation.
  • Court costs are added by the court system and are largely uniform across the state.
  • Some resolutions of a case may change the fine, but costs often remain part of the total.

We do not state a dollar figure for North Carolina court costs, because our sourced figures do not include one. The clerk of court can give you the current total for your case.

How many points does driving without a license add?

North Carolina uses two separate point systems. The first is license points, kept by the DMV. The second is insurance points (SDIP), which insurers use to set premiums.

For reference, a typical speeding ticket carries 2–3 license points. Our sourced figures do not give a point value for driving without a license, so we do not state one. Check with the North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles for how your specific conviction is treated.

SDIP points work differently. They can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier. A conviction that adds SDIP points can therefore cost far more over time than the fine.

License points and SDIP points are separate. Ask both the DMV and your insurer how a conviction is recorded.

Does driving without a license raise insurance?

It can. Nationally, moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, and your own change could be higher or lower.

In North Carolina, the SDIP system adds a second layer. Insurance points can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier, so the effect depends on how your insurer applies them.

Whether a no-license conviction counts as a moving violation for rating purposes is a question for your insurer. We do not recommend any insurer or give insurance advice.

  • Ask your insurer how a conviction would be treated before you decide how to resolve the case.
  • Ask how long a conviction can affect your rate.
  • Compare the possible premium change with the fine and court costs.

Can it be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes cases are dismissed or resolved differently, but outcomes depend on the facts and on the court. We cannot predict what a North Carolina court will do with your case.

Questions worth raising with the clerk of court, the prosecutor, or a licensed attorney of your choosing:

  • Whether proof that you held a valid license at the time matters to the charge.
  • Whether the charge could be resolved with a different outcome than a conviction.
  • Whether any resolution changes court costs, license points or SDIP points.

Because the charge is often a misdemeanor, the stakes can go beyond money. A criminal record can matter for work and other purposes. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and does not give legal advice or recommend attorneys.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the citation?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can treat a missed appearance or unpaid amount as a separate problem, and a misdemeanor charge does not go away on its own.

Possible consequences vary by court and can include additional penalties, a hold on your driving record, or further court action. We do not state specific deadlines or added amounts because our sourced figures do not include them.

Read the court date and instructions on your citation. If you cannot appear or pay on time, contact the clerk of court before the date passes and ask what options exist.

The citation is the most reliable source for your court, your date and the amount set for your case.
