How much is a driving without a license ticket in South Carolina?

Nationally, a first offense for driving without a license commonly runs $50–$500, and it is often chargeable as a misdemeanor. In South Carolina the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (South Carolina DMV).

We do not list a single South Carolina dollar figure for this offense, because the amount depends on the court that handles your case and on how the offense is written up. The national range is a guide to the typical scale, not a statement of what a South Carolina court will order.

The number on your citation, plus the statewide assessments and surcharges, is what you should plan around. The national range alone will understate the total.

How is the fine set?

The fine for this violation in South Carolina is set by the court within the state's traffic code. That means the amount is not a fixed number we can quote for every driver. It is the figure the court places on your citation.

Several things can matter in practice:

  • The exact charge written on the citation.
  • Whether it is charged as a misdemeanor, which is common nationally.
  • Whether it is a first offense or a repeat.
  • The court that hears the case.

The state's traffic code is available through South Carolina statutes via Justia. Read the section named on your citation rather than relying on a general summary.

Why is the total higher than the fine?

Statewide assessments and surcharges are added to every fine, and they usually exceed it. So the amount you actually pay can be well above the fine line on the citation.

Ask the court clerk for the full amount due, not just the base fine. The clerk can tell you what is included and what payment methods the court accepts.

This is also why comparing your ticket to a national range can mislead. The national $50–$500 range describes typical fines, not the total after South Carolina's added costs.

How many points does it add in South Carolina?

South Carolina's points system assigns 2–6 points by mph over, and 12 points means suspension. Points halve after 12 months (South Carolina DMV).

That scale is described by speed, so it does not tell us how many points, if any, apply to a driving without a license charge. Whether your citation carries points is something to confirm with the court or the South Carolina DMV.

If points do apply, they matter in two ways. They add toward the 12-point suspension threshold, and a conviction can show on your driving record.

Does driving without a license raise insurance rates?

Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a South Carolina quote.

Whether this specific charge counts as a moving violation for your insurer depends on how it is recorded and on the insurer's own rules. Some drivers in this situation also have no active policy, which changes the picture further.

TicketCostGuide does not recommend insurers or give insurance advice. If you are worried about your rate, ask your insurer how it treats this type of conviction.

Can it be dismissed or reduced?

Outcomes depend on the court, the facts and the charge. We cannot say what a particular South Carolina court will do.

Practical steps drivers commonly take:

  1. Read the citation closely. Confirm the charge, the court and the response instructions.
  2. Check your license status with the South Carolina DMV. A license that was valid, expired, suspended or never issued can lead to different charges.
  3. Contact the court clerk and ask what options exist and what documents the court accepts.
  4. If the charge is a misdemeanor, consider speaking with a licensed attorney. We do not recommend any specific attorney.

This is general information, not legal advice.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can add penalties, and unresolved cases can lead to further action against your driving privileges or a warrant, depending on the court and the charge.

If the offense is charged as a misdemeanor, as is often the case nationally, it is more serious than a routine fine. Do not assume it will go away on its own.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court clerk before the deadline on your citation. Ask whether the court offers any payment arrangement.
