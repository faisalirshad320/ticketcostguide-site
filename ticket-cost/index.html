How much does a traffic ticket cost in the US?

There is no single national price for a ticket. Each state sets its own penalties by statute, and counties and courts add their own costs on top. That is why the same violation can cost very different amounts in two places.

The broad pattern is consistent, though. Seatbelt and equipment tickets sit at the bottom, from about $10–$50. Most ordinary moving violations land between $50 and $500 with costs. Criminal offenses like reckless driving reach $500–$2,500 (Governors Highway Safety Association).

Think of the cost as three tiers:

  • Low tier: seatbelt, child seat, window tint, and other equipment or safety-device tickets.
  • Middle tier: red light, stop sign, failure to yield, texting, careless driving, and most speeding.
  • Top tier: criminal offenses such as reckless driving and driving on a suspended license.

The table above lists the locked range for each category. Treat every figure as a typical range, not a quote. Your citation, or the court or clerk that issued it, gives the amount you actually owe.

All ranges on this page vary by state. Where an amount also depends on the county or court, we give only the typical range and never an exact local figure.

Which tickets cost the most?

The costliest tickets are the ones that count as crimes rather than infractions. Reckless driving can reach up to $2,500, and it is a criminal charge. A suspended license commonly runs $200–$2,500+ and is criminal in every state.

No insurance is the other big one. The range is $100–$1,500+, and it can come with an impound and an SR-22 requirement. An SR-22 is a form an insurer files to prove you carry coverage. Requirements and how long they last depend on your state.

Here is how the expensive categories compare:

  • Reckless driving: up to $2,500 (criminal).
  • Suspended license: commonly $200–$2,500+ (criminal in every state).
  • No insurance: $100–$1,500+ plus possible impound/SR-22.

A fine is not the only cost for these offenses. Criminal charges can bring a court appearance and a record, and the fine is only one part of the total. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and cannot advise on a specific charge. If you face a criminal citation, a licensed attorney in your state is the right person to ask.

Most everyday tickets cost much less. Red light and stop sign tickets typically run $50–$500, and failure to yield is the same range. Careless driving commonly falls between $100 and $500.

Which tickets are the cheapest?

Equipment and safety-device tickets are usually the cheapest. A seatbelt ticket typically costs $10–$200 depending on the state, with the lowest end from about $10–$50. A child car seat ticket is higher, at $25–$500, because states treat child restraints more seriously.

Window tint tickets run $25–$250 and are often correctable. Expired registration runs $25–$250, but DMV late penalties can be added on top.

Two cautions apply to the cheap tier:

  • A low fine does not always mean a low total. Court costs and DMV penalties are separate and can raise what you pay.
  • Ignoring a small ticket can turn it into a bigger problem. Missed deadlines can bring added penalties, and in some states a license hold. The rules depend on your state and court.

If you got a ticket in this tier, read it closely. Many equipment violations have a way to fix the problem and have the ticket reduced or dismissed. See the section on dismissals below.

Which violations add points, and which are moving violations?

A moving violation happens while the vehicle is in motion. Red light, stop sign, failure to yield, texting while driving, careless driving, reckless driving and speeding are all moving violations. A non-moving violation relates to the vehicle or paperwork, such as expired registration, window tint or equipment problems.

Points are usually tied to moving violations. Whether a violation carries points, and how many, is set by each state. We do not list point values here, because they vary by state and we only publish figures we can source. Your state DMV publishes its own point schedule.

The distinction still matters for cost. Moving violations are more likely to be reported to your driving record, and that can affect your license status and insurance. Non-moving violations are more often a one-time fine, though unpaid ones can cause trouble.

Some categories fall in between. Driving without a license is commonly $50–$500 for a first offense, but how a state classifies it varies. Suspended license is criminal in every state.

Check your state's DMV or court website for how your citation is classified before you decide how to respond.

Why is the total more than the listed fine?

The fine is the base penalty. The total on your ticket often includes court costs and surcharges added by the state, county or court. The primary range on this page reflects this, since most ordinary moving violations land between $50 and $500 with costs (Governors Highway Safety Association).

Some violations carry named add-ons beyond the fine:

  • Expired registration: $25–$250 plus DMV late penalties.
  • No insurance: $100–$1,500+ plus possible impound/SR-22.
  • School-zone and work-zone speeding: commonly double the base fine.

Added costs vary by county and court. Two drivers with the same violation in different counties can owe different totals. We do not publish an exact local figure, because it changes by jurisdiction.

The most reliable way to learn what you owe is to read the citation, then check the court's payment page or call the clerk. The clerk can tell you the amount due and the deadline. They cannot give legal advice, but they can explain the process.

Pay attention to the deadline. A late payment can add penalties, and an unpaid ticket can create bigger problems later. Rules vary by state.

Do school-zone and work-zone tickets cost more?

Yes. School-zone and work-zone speeding commonly cost double the base fine. States raise penalties in these areas because the people nearby, such as children, crossing guards and road crews, are exposed to traffic.

Speed is a well-documented safety issue. For background on why speed limits matter, see the federal overview from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration and the research summaries from the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety. Both explain the safety reasoning, not what your local fine will be.

A few practical points:

  • Doubling applies to the base fine. Court costs and surcharges may still be added.
  • Whether doubling applies depends on the state, and sometimes on whether the zone was active, such as when workers are present or the school-zone signs are in effect.
  • Some states treat these zones as more serious in other ways too. Check your state's rules.

Because the base fine varies by state, county and court, the doubled amount does too. Use your citation for the real number.

Which tickets raise insurance, and which can be dismissed?

Insurance. Insurers set their own rates, and TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer or predict a rate change. In general, moving violations and criminal offenses are more likely to affect your record, and a record can influence what insurers charge. A no-insurance ticket can also bring an SR-22 requirement. Equipment tickets are less likely to matter, but this varies by state and by insurer.

Dismissal. Some tickets are easier to resolve than others. Window tint is often correctable, meaning you can fix the problem and show proof to the court. Equipment and registration tickets may be handled the same way in some places. Whether a ticket can be dismissed, reduced or corrected depends on the state and court.

Criminal offenses are different. Reckless driving, which can reach up to $2,500, and a suspended license, which is criminal in every state, usually require a court appearance. These are not tickets to handle without understanding the consequences.

TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and does not give legal advice. For a criminal charge or any question about your rights, talk to a licensed attorney in your state.

For minor tickets, the court clerk or the court's website will list your options, deadlines and any correction process.

Frequently asked questions

How much does the average traffic ticket cost?

Most ordinary moving violations land between $50 and $500 with costs. Equipment and seatbelt tickets can start around $10–$50, and criminal offenses like reckless driving run $500–$2,500. The exact amount varies by state, county and court, so your citation is the best source.

How much is a red light or stop sign ticket?

A red light ticket typically costs $50–$500, and a stop sign ticket is the same range. The exact amount varies by state, and county or court costs may be added. Your citation or the court clerk can confirm the total you owe.

How much is a ticket for no insurance?

No insurance commonly costs $100–$1,500+ and can come with a possible impound and an SR-22 requirement. The amount and the consequences vary by state. Check your citation and your state's rules, and consider asking a licensed attorney if you are unsure.

How much is a reckless driving ticket?

Reckless driving can cost up to $2,500 and is a criminal offense. Criminal charges often involve a court appearance and consequences beyond the fine. The details vary by state, so a licensed attorney in your state is the right person to advise you.

Is a suspended license ticket a criminal offense?

Yes. Driving on a suspended license is criminal in every state, and fines commonly run $200–$2,500+. Other consequences vary by state. Because it is a criminal matter, consider speaking with a licensed attorney rather than handling it alone.

Do school-zone and work-zone tickets cost more?

Yes. School-zone and work-zone speeding commonly cost double the base fine. The base fine varies by state, county and court, so the doubled amount does too. Your citation shows the exact amount, and the court clerk can explain any added costs.

Can a window tint or equipment ticket be dismissed?

Window tint tickets, which run $25–$250, are often correctable, meaning you may fix the issue and show proof to the court. Whether that leads to a dismissal or reduction depends on your state and court. Check your citation for instructions.
