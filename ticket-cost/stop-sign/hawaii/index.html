How much is a stop sign ticket in Hawaii?

Nationally, a stop sign ticket runs $50–$500 depending on the state. In Hawaii the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Hawaii Department of Transportation).

We do not publish a single Hawaii dollar amount, because the number that matters is the one printed on your citation or assigned by the court. The national range is a context figure only. It is not a promise about what a Hawaii court will charge.

Your citation, and any notice from the court, is the authority on what you owe. Treat any other figure, including ours, as background.

The base fine is also not the whole bill. Court fees and assessments are added by the district court, so the total you pay can be higher than the fine alone.

How is the stop sign fine set in Hawaii?

The fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. That means it is not a number you can look up in a simple national chart and trust.

The traffic code itself is published online. You can browse Hawaii statutes through Hawaii statutes via Justia to read the rules on stop signs and the penalty provisions that apply.

In practice, three things shape what you are asked to pay:

  • The violation charged on the citation.
  • The amount the court sets within the traffic code.
  • Court fees and assessments added by the district court.

If your citation lists an amount, that is where to start. If it does not, the court listed on the citation can tell you what applies to your case.

How many points does a stop sign ticket carry in Hawaii?

None. Hawaii has no demerit-point system, so a stop sign conviction does not add points to your record the way it would in many other states.

That does not mean convictions are harmless. In Hawaii, repeat convictions and excessive-speeding convictions drive license action directly, rather than through a point total.

  • No points are counted toward a threshold.
  • Repeated convictions can matter on their own.
  • License action follows from the convictions, not from a running score.

If you have prior convictions, a new one may carry more weight than the fine suggests. Anyone with a record worth worrying about may want to speak with a licensed attorney, which is a decision for you. We do not recommend a specific attorney.

Does a stop sign ticket raise insurance in Hawaii?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Hawaii-specific or insurer-specific number.

Whether your own rate changes depends on your insurer, your driving history and how the ticket is resolved. A stop sign violation is generally treated as a moving violation, but each company sets its own rules.

The ~20% figure is a national average. Your premium could rise by more, by less or not at all.

If you are weighing how to handle the ticket, ask your insurer how it treats a conviction compared with other outcomes. We do not recommend any insurer and cannot predict your quote.

Can a stop sign ticket be dismissed or reduced in Hawaii?

Sometimes, but we cannot promise any outcome. The court that handles your citation decides, and the process is set out on the citation and in notices from the court.

Typical options people look at include:

  1. Paying the amount on the citation, which resolves the ticket but may result in a conviction.
  2. Contesting the citation and presenting your side to the court.
  3. Contacting the court to ask what choices are available for your case.

Read the instructions on your citation carefully. They explain how to respond, and missing a step can limit your choices.

This page is general information, not legal advice. If a conviction would put your license or job at risk, consider talking to a licensed attorney of your own choosing.

What happens if I don't pay a stop sign ticket in Hawaii?

Ignoring a ticket usually makes things worse. Because the district court adds fees and assessments, delays can leave you with a larger total or additional court action.

The specific consequences for unpaid or unanswered citations are set by the court and by state law. They can affect more than the money owed, so do not assume the ticket will simply go away.

  • Check the response instructions on your citation.
  • Contact the court listed on it if you are unsure what to do.
  • Keep copies of anything you send or receive.

Because Hawaii has no demerit-point system, license problems tend to come from repeat convictions rather than from a point total. A single unresolved ticket on top of earlier convictions can still complicate matters.
