How much is a stop sign ticket in California?

Nationally, stop sign tickets run $50–$500 depending on the state. In California, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (California Department of Motor Vehicles).

The locked California figure is that a stop-sign violation totals about $238 with penalty assessments. That is a total, not the bare base fine.

Your own bill can differ from that figure. County and court fees vary, so the amount printed on your citation or shown on the court's website is the one that counts.

The $50–$500 range is a national range. It is not a California price list, and we do not give an exact California base fine because the court sets it.

How is the fine set, and why is the total so much higher than the base fine?

The base fine for a stop sign violation is set within the state's traffic code and applied by the court handling your citation. The court shows the amount due on the citation or in its records.

The base fine is not the whole cost. Penalty assessments, county fees and surcharges multiply the base fine roughly 4–5×.

That is why the California stop-sign total of about $238 with penalty assessments looks so much larger than a base fine alone. If you are comparing quotes or reading older articles, check whether they show the base fine or the total.

  • Base fine: set within the state's traffic code.
  • Add-ons: penalty assessments, county fees and surcharges.
  • What you pay: the total shown by the court, which can vary by county and court.

How many points does a stop sign ticket add in California?

California uses a point system tracked by the DMV. Our locked figures describe it this way: 1 point for ordinary speeding (2 points at 100+ mph), and 4 points in 12 months = suspension under the Negligent Operator Treatment System.

Those figures are for speeding. We do not state a stop sign point value here because it is not among the figures we have verified for this page. Check your record or the DMV for the count that applies to your conviction (California Department of Motor Vehicles).

The 4-points-in-12-months threshold still matters. A stop sign conviction that adds points can bring you closer to it if you have other violations in the same period.

Points usually come from a conviction, not from the citation alone. How you resolve the ticket can change what lands on your record.

Does a stop sign ticket raise your insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums ~20% on average. That is a national figure, not a California quote.

Your own change depends on your insurer, your driving record and how the ticket is resolved. We do not recommend any insurer or predict what yours will do.

When you weigh the cost of a ticket, look past the court total. A premium increase can last longer than the one-time payment, so it belongs in the comparison.

Asking your insurer how it treats a conviction for a moving violation, before you decide how to handle the citation, is a reasonable step.

Can a stop sign ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes. We are not a law firm and cannot tell you whether your ticket qualifies. These are the general paths people look at:

  • Pay it: usually resolves the case, but it generally means a conviction and possible points.
  • Contest it: you can ask the court to hear your case. The court decides the outcome.
  • Ask about alternatives: some courts offer options such as traffic school or payment arrangements. Availability depends on the court and your record.

Options and deadlines are on the citation and the court's website. Because county and court fees vary, ask the court what any option would cost in total.

For advice on your specific case, speak with a licensed attorney of your own choosing.

What happens if I don't pay a stop sign ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can add fees and penalties for missed deadlines, and the surcharge structure already multiplies the base fine roughly 4–5×.

An unresolved ticket can also lead to trouble with your driver's license. The DMV explains how court notices affect license status (California Department of Motor Vehicles).

If you cannot pay the full amount by the deadline, contact the court before the deadline passes and ask what it allows. We do not state specific deadlines here because they are set on your citation and by your court.

Read the citation carefully. It lists the court, the amount due and the dates that apply to you.
