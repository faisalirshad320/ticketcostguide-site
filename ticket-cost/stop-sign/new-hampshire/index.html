How much is a stop sign ticket in New Hampshire?

Nationally, a stop sign ticket costs $50–$500 depending on the state. In New Hampshire, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles).

That means there is no single number we can responsibly print for every driver. The figure on your paperwork is the one that matters.

The national range is a reference point only. It combines very different state schedules, so a New Hampshire ticket may land anywhere in or near it, and we do not guess where.

Bottom line: your citation shows the amount set by the court. Add the 24% penalty assessment to understand what the full cost is likely to be.

How is the fine set, and what is the 24% penalty assessment?

The fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The court sets the base amount, and it is printed on or tied to your citation.

On top of that base fine, a 24% penalty assessment is added by statute to every base fine. This is a percentage, so a higher base fine means a larger assessment in dollars.

To estimate your total, multiply the base fine on your citation by 1.24. If the citation already lists a total, check whether the assessment is included before you pay.

You can read the state's traffic provisions through New Hampshire statutes via Justia. The statutes are the legal source; the citation and the court are where your actual amount comes from.

  • Base fine: set by the court on the citation.
  • Penalty assessment: 24% added to every base fine.
  • Total: base fine plus the assessment.

How many points is a stop sign ticket in New Hampshire?

New Hampshire's points system assigns 3–4 points by offense. The number you receive depends on the offense that is actually charged and entered on your record.

We do not assign a specific point value to a stop sign violation here, because the locked range depends on how the offense is classified. Check your citation and your driving record with the New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles.

Points matter because they add up. Under the state's rule, 12 points in 12 months can lead to a suspension of up to 3 months.

One ticket on its own does not reach the suspension threshold. The risk comes from combining it with other violations in the same 12-month window.

Does a stop sign ticket raise insurance in New Hampshire?

It can. Nationally, moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a New Hampshire-specific one.

Whether your own rate changes depends on your insurer, your history and whether the ticket ends in a conviction. We do not recommend or compare insurers, and we cannot predict your quote.

Some practical points to keep in mind:

  • The increase is tied to a conviction on your record, not to receiving the citation alone.
  • An average is not a promise. Your result may be higher, lower or zero.
  • Your policy documents or your insurer can tell you how a moving violation is treated.

When weighing options, compare the one-time fine with the possible cost of higher premiums over time, not just the fine.

What should I do first after getting a stop sign ticket?

Start by reading the citation carefully. It shows the offense charged, the court that will handle it and the amount set by that court.

  1. Confirm the offense and the court listed on the citation.
  2. Note any response or payment deadline printed on it. We do not state deadlines here because they come from the citation and the court.
  3. Work out the total by adding the 24% penalty assessment to the base fine.
  4. Check how many points the charged offense carries, which is 3–4 points by offense.
  5. Decide whether to pay or contest, knowing that payment is typically treated as accepting the violation.

If you are unsure, the court clerk can explain procedure. A licensed attorney can give legal advice. TicketCostGuide does not give legal advice or recommend attorneys.

Can a stop sign ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Possibly, but that is up to the court, not to this guide. The locked facts we rely on do not describe a specific dismissal or reduction program in New Hampshire, so we do not claim one exists.

In general, drivers facing a traffic citation have a few broad paths:

  • Pay: this resolves the matter but typically means accepting the violation and any resulting points.
  • Contest: you ask the court to hear the case rather than paying.
  • Ask the court: the clerk can tell you what options the court offers for your citation.

Before choosing, compare the total cost with the possible points and insurance effects. A reduced fine is not always a better outcome if the conviction still goes on your record.

For advice on your specific case, speak with a licensed attorney. We do not recommend any specific attorney.

What happens if I don't pay a stop sign ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. The amount you owe still carries the 24% penalty assessment, and unresolved tickets can lead to further court action.

We do not state specific late fees, deadlines or penalties here, because those are not part of the locked figures we use. Your citation and the court will tell you what applies.

An unresolved ticket can also interfere with your driving privileges. Separately, if points build up to 12 in 12 months, a suspension of up to 3 months is possible.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court listed on the citation before any deadline passes and ask what it allows.
