How much is a stop sign ticket in the US?

A stop sign ticket costs $50–$500 depending on the state (Governors Highway Safety Association). That is the national range we use as our lead figure. It comes from state statutes and schedules, last reviewed Oct 2026.

The range is wide because there is no single national fine. Each state decides what a stop sign violation costs, and some leave room for the county or court to set the final figure. Where that is the case, the exact amount varies by county and court, and we give only the range rather than guess at a local number.

The amount printed on your citation is often not the full amount you pay. Several items can sit on top of the base fine:

  • Penalty assessments or surcharges. Some states add these to every fine. California is the clearest example, covered below.
  • Court costs. These are added in most states, so the total can be higher than the base fine.
  • Optional or conditional fees. Traffic school, payment plans and late payment charges all vary by state and court.

The practical point is that two drivers who ran the same stop sign in different places can owe very different amounts. A driver in one state may pay near the bottom of the range. A driver in another may pay near the top, before any extra costs.

Read your citation and the court's payment instructions. The number the court asks for on your due date is the number that counts, not a figure from a general guide.

How much is a stop sign ticket in California?

California's stop-sign violation totals about $238 with penalty assessments. This is one of our locked examples, drawn from state statutes and schedules, last reviewed Oct 2026 (Governors Highway Safety Association).

The important phrase is "with penalty assessments." California builds a number of add-ons into the amount a driver actually pays. The base fine on its own is a smaller figure, and the total is what shows up on the bail schedule and the payment notice. We do not break out the individual add-ons here because we only publish figures we can source to the locked data.

Treat the California figure as an example of how a state fine grows once surcharges are added. It is not a prediction for other states. A state with no large penalty assessments may produce a total closer to its base fine, while another may stack court costs and surcharges differently.

A few cautions apply even in California:

  • The figure is an approximation, so the amount on your own citation may differ.
  • Court costs and fees for things like traffic school or installment plans are not part of the $238 figure and vary by court.
  • Rules and schedules are updated by the state, so check the current schedule for your county court.

If you want to read the underlying law, state codes are collected by Justia (State codes via Justia). Look up your state's vehicle code and find the section on stop signs.

Is a rolling stop treated the same as running a stop sign?

In most cases, yes. Rolling-stop citations, often called a 'California stop', are charged under the same statutes as any other stop sign violation. That means the fine range is the same: $50–$500 depending on the state.

Many drivers assume a slow roll through an empty intersection is a lesser offense. The law usually does not draw that line. Most stop sign statutes require a complete stop, and an officer who sees the vehicle keep moving can cite the driver for failing to stop.

Because the charge is the same, the consequences are the same too. A rolling-stop ticket can carry the same fine, the same court costs, and the same point value in states with a point system. It can also appear on your driving record in the same way as a full run-through.

What often differs is the evidence. A driver who rolled slowly may have a stronger argument about visibility, the position of the sign, or what the officer could actually see. We cover those angles in the dismissal section. They are arguments, not guarantees, and results vary by court.

The name 'California stop' is slang. It does not mean the ticket is a California ticket, and it does not mean the penalty is lower. Your state's own statute and schedule decide the cost.

How many points does a stop sign ticket add to my license?

Where point systems exist, the typical point value for a stop sign violation is 2–4. That figure is part of our locked data, last reviewed Oct 2026.

Not every state uses points. Some track violations on the driving record without assigning a numeric score, and others use their own scoring method. If your state has no point system, the 2–4 range does not apply to you. The exact value in your state varies by state, so check the motor vehicle agency or the statute.

Points matter because they accumulate. In states that use them, enough points inside a set period can lead to consequences such as a warning letter, a required class, or a license suspension. The thresholds and time windows vary by state, and we do not state them here because they are not part of our locked data.

Things to check on your own record:

  • Whether your state assigns points to a stop sign violation at all.
  • How long the points stay on your record, which varies by state.
  • Whether a court-approved class or a deferral can keep points off, which varies by state and court.

If you already have other violations, a stop sign ticket may push you closer to a threshold. That is a reason to read the options on your citation before you pay, since paying is usually treated as an admission of the violation.

Does a stop sign ticket raise car insurance rates?

It can, but the effect varies by insurer, by state and by your driving history. We do not publish a dollar figure for the increase because we have no locked data to support one.

Insurers typically look at your motor vehicle record when you renew or shop for a new policy. A moving violation that shows up there may be weighed along with other factors. A single stop sign ticket on an otherwise clean record is generally viewed differently from several violations in a short period, but how each company treats it varies.

Several things can change the outcome:

  • Whether the conviction is on your record. A dismissal or a deferral that keeps the violation off may avoid the issue. Availability varies by state and court.
  • Points. In states with a point system, the typical 2–4 points can be one signal insurers see.
  • Your insurer's own rules. Some companies have forgiveness policies. Others do not. Ask your insurer directly.

The fine is a one-time cost. The insurance effect can last longer, which is why some drivers weigh the total cost of a ticket instead of only the fine. We do not recommend any insurer or tell you which policy to buy.

Before you pay, find out whether paying counts as a conviction in your state. If it does, the ticket may reach your record and your insurer.

Can a stop sign ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes. Whether a court dismisses or reduces a stop sign ticket depends on the state, the court and the facts. We cannot say how often it happens, and nothing here is legal advice.

Drivers commonly look at a few routes, all of which vary by state and court:

  • Contesting the ticket. You plead not guilty and the officer has to show the violation. Common questions include whether the sign was visible, blocked or missing, and whether the officer had a clear view of the intersection.
  • Traffic school or a similar class. Some courts allow a class in exchange for keeping the violation off your record or avoiding points. Eligibility rules and fees vary.
  • Deferral or diversion. A court may hold the case for a period and dismiss it if you stay out of trouble. This is not available everywhere.
  • Reduction to a lesser charge. A prosecutor or judge may agree to a non-moving or lower-level violation. This is up to the court.

A rolling-stop ticket is charged under the same statutes as a full run-through, so a rolling stop is not a defense on its own. Arguments about the sign or the officer's view are separate matters for the court to weigh.

Read the citation for the deadline and the court's instructions on how to respond. Missing the deadline can remove options that were available earlier. If you want personal advice, you would need to speak with a licensed attorney in your state. We do not recommend a specific one.

What happens if I don't pay a stop sign ticket?

Ignoring the ticket usually makes things worse. The exact consequences vary by state and court, but they commonly grow over time.

Court costs are added in most states, and a late payment can bring extra fees on top of the original amount. A fine that started somewhere in the $50–$500 depending on the state range can end up higher by the time it is resolved. We do not publish late fee amounts because they vary by court.

Other consequences that states may use include:

  • A failure-to-appear or failure-to-pay notice added to your case.
  • A hold or suspension on your driver's license until the matter is settled.
  • A block on renewing your vehicle registration in some places.
  • Referral of the debt for collection.
  • A warrant in some situations, depending on how the state treats the original ticket.

If you cannot afford the full amount, contact the court clerk before the due date. Many courts offer payment plans, extensions or community service options. Availability varies by court, and asking early is better than waiting until the deadline has passed.

Not responding is not the same as contesting. If you want to fight the ticket, you have to respond to the court in the way the citation describes.
