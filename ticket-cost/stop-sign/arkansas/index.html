How much is a stop sign ticket in Arkansas?

Nationally, a stop sign ticket runs $50–$500 depending on the state. In Arkansas, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Arkansas Department of Finance and Administration — Driver Services).

The national range is not an Arkansas price. We do not publish a single Arkansas dollar figure because the court that handles your ticket decides the amount within the state's traffic code.

The number printed on your citation, or the amount the court clerk quotes, is the one that matters for your case.

Quick take: the $50–$500 range is national. Your Arkansas amount is whatever the court sets on your citation, plus court costs.

How is the fine set, and what are court costs?

Arkansas traffic violations are handled in court, and the court sets the fine on the citation within the state's traffic code. You can read the statutes through Arkansas statutes via Justia.

On top of the fine, court costs are added by the district court and vary by county. That is why the total you pay can differ from what a friend paid in another county for the same violation.

When you look at your citation or the court's notice, check for these items:

  • The fine amount for the stop sign violation.
  • Any court costs or surcharges added by the district court.
  • The total due and the court's accepted payment methods.
  • The court date or the date your payment is due.

If the total looks different from what you expected, ask the clerk to itemize the fine and the court costs. Clerks can explain the breakdown but cannot give legal advice.

How many points does a stop sign ticket add in Arkansas?

The Arkansas points system assigns 3–8 points by speed, and 14+ points can trigger suspension. Those are the figures we have sourced for the state's system.

A stop sign violation is not a speeding offense, so the "by speed" scale does not map neatly onto it. We do not state a specific point value for a stop sign conviction.

To find out how your conviction would be scored, contact Arkansas Department of Finance and Administration — Driver Services. They keep the driving record and can tell you how a conviction is recorded.

Points matter mostly through accumulation. One ticket on its own is far from the suspension threshold, but several convictions can add up. Knowing your current point total helps you decide whether a single ticket is worth contesting.

Does a stop sign ticket raise your insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not an Arkansas-specific one.

Whether your own rate changes depends on your insurer, your driving history and how the conviction appears on your record. We do not recommend or compare insurers.

A few points to keep in mind:

  • The effect is usually tied to a conviction, not to receiving a citation.
  • Insurers often look at the whole record, so a clean history may matter.
  • A rate change usually shows up at renewal, so the cost may arrive months after the court payment.

Over time, the insurance effect can outweigh the fine itself. That is worth weighing when deciding how to respond to the citation.

The ~20% figure is a US average for moving-violation convictions. Your own change could be higher, lower or none.

Can a stop sign ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but the outcome depends on the court and the facts. The court sets the fine on the citation, and the district court handles the case, so options are decided locally.

People commonly look at these routes:

  • Paying the ticket. This usually resolves the case but may mean a conviction on your record.
  • Contesting it. You can ask for a hearing and present your side, such as a blocked or missing sign.
  • Asking the court about alternatives. Some courts may offer options on certain tickets. Ask the clerk what your court allows.

We cannot say which option fits your situation, and we do not recommend attorneys. For legal advice about your specific ticket, consult a licensed Arkansas attorney of your choosing.

Because the points and insurance effects can last longer than the fine, it helps to understand what each option does to your record before deciding.

What happens if I don't pay a stop sign ticket?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can add consequences when a ticket goes unpaid or a court date is missed, and those can include extra costs or a license problem.

We do not state specific deadlines or penalty amounts for Arkansas, because we have not sourced them for this page. Your citation and the court's notice list the dates that apply to you.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court clerk before the due date. Clerks can tell you what options the court offers.

Also keep in mind the points system. Because 14+ points can trigger suspension, an unresolved ticket on top of a record with other convictions is a bigger risk than a single ticket alone. To check your own record, contact Arkansas Department of Finance and Administration — Driver Services.

What should I do after getting a stop sign ticket?

Start by reading the citation closely. Confirm the violation listed, the court that will hear it and the date or deadline to respond.

  1. Note the court name and county, since court costs vary by county.
  2. Check the fine amount, or ask the clerk for it, because the court sets it on the citation.
  3. Look up your current points through Driver Services if you are near the 14+ point suspension threshold.
  4. Think about the insurance side, since convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average.
  5. Decide whether to pay, contest, or ask the court about options.

Keep a copy of the citation and any receipt after you pay. If you contest the ticket, write down details such as the location, the signage and the conditions while you still remember them.
