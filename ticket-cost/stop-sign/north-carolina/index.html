How much is a stop sign ticket in North Carolina?

Nationally, stop sign fines run $50–$500 depending on the state. In North Carolina, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles).

We do not list a single North Carolina dollar figure here, because the amount you owe is the one printed on your citation or confirmed by the court. Any page that gives one exact statewide number for every driver is guessing.

The $50–$500 range is a national range across states. It is not a North Carolina schedule, and your ticket may fall inside or outside it.

The fine is only part of the bill. The next section explains why court costs matter just as much.

How is the fine and total cost set?

The fine for a stop sign violation in North Carolina is set by the court within the state's traffic code. You can read the underlying law through the North Carolina statutes via Justia. The code defines the offense, and the court applies it to your case.

On top of the fine, uniform statewide court costs apply. On most tickets, those court costs dwarf the fine. That means a ticket with a modest fine can still produce a much larger total.

When you look at your paperwork, check for these items:

  • The fine amount for the violation.
  • Court costs and any surcharges added to it.
  • The court date or payment instructions.
  • Whether the citation says you may pay without appearing.

If the total is unclear, the court clerk is the right place to ask what is owed. The clerk can confirm the amount but cannot give legal advice.

How many points does a stop sign ticket carry in North Carolina?

Our locked reference data covers license points for typical speeding: 2–3 points. It does not give a separate stop sign point value, and we will not guess one.

Two systems matter, and people often mix them up:

  • License points are recorded by the state's driver licensing agency. See the North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles for how they apply to your record.
  • Insurance (SDIP) points are separate. They are used for insurance pricing and can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier.

Because the two systems are separate, a ticket can affect your insurance rating even when the license-point effect looks small. Check both before you decide how to respond.

Do not assume a stop sign ticket carries the same points as speeding. Ask the DMV or read the official point schedule for the violation on your citation.

Does a stop sign ticket raise insurance rates?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a North Carolina quote, and your own change could be higher, lower or zero.

The separate insurance point system (SDIP) can raise premiums 30%+ per point tier. Whether your insurer applies it, and how, depends on the outcome of the ticket and on the insurer's own rules.

A few practical points:

  • The conviction is usually what matters to an insurer, not the citation alone.
  • How the case is resolved can change how it appears on your record.
  • Your insurer, not this guide, can tell you how your premium would change.

We do not recommend any insurer. If a renewal is coming up, ask your current insurer how a conviction would be treated.

Can a stop sign ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes, but outcomes depend on the facts and on the court. We cannot predict whether yours would be dismissed or reduced.

Common reasons people contest a stop sign ticket include:

  • The sign was missing, blocked or hard to see.
  • The officer's view of the intersection was limited.
  • The citation contains an error in the location, date or vehicle.

None of these guarantees a result. Because the fine is set by the court, any change to the amount or the charge is also up to the court.

If you want advice on your specific case, consider speaking with a licensed North Carolina attorney. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and does not give legal advice.

What happens if I don't pay a stop sign ticket?

Ignoring a ticket usually makes things worse. Courts can add consequences when a citation goes unanswered, and those can affect your driving record and your license.

The exact steps depend on the court and on what the citation says. Look at the instructions on the ticket and contact the clerk before any deadline passes.

If you cannot pay the full amount, ask the clerk what options exist. Courts differ in whether they offer payment arrangements, so do not assume one is available.

Ask the court for the total owed, including court costs, before you pay. The fine alone is not the full amount.

What are my options after getting a stop sign ticket?

Most drivers choose between paying the ticket and contesting it. Each choice has trade-offs.

  1. Pay it. This is usually quick, but it may count as a conviction. That can matter for both license points and insurance.
  2. Contest it. You ask the court to hear the case. This takes more time and the result is not guaranteed.
  3. Ask about alternatives. Some courts may offer options that change how the ticket is recorded. Availability varies, so ask the clerk or review the court's published rules.

Before you decide, compare the total cost with the possible insurance effect. A national average of about 20% on premiums can add up over time, though your own result may differ.
