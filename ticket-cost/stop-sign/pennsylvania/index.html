How much is a stop sign ticket in Pennsylvania?

Nationally, a stop sign ticket costs $50–$500 depending on the state. In Pennsylvania the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services).

We do not print a single Pennsylvania dollar figure here, because the amount you owe is the one written on your citation or shown by the court handling your case. The national range is context only. It is not a Pennsylvania price.

The $50–$500 range is a national figure. Your Pennsylvania amount comes from the court on your citation.

The fine line is also not the whole bill. See the next section for how costs and surcharges add up.

How is the fine set, and why is the total higher than the fine?

In Pennsylvania the fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. The citation is where you find the amount tied to your case. The code itself is published online through Pennsylvania statutes via Justia.

Court costs, the EMS surcharge and other statutory fees typically exceed the base fine. That means the total you pay can be noticeably larger than the fine alone.

When you compare a number you saw online with your own ticket, check whether it is the fine only or the full amount including costs. Many ticket-cost pages quote only the base fine, which understates the real payment.

  • Look for the fine amount on the citation.
  • Look for separate lines for court costs and surcharges.
  • Ask the court office which total it will accept if you plead guilty and pay.

How many points does a stop sign ticket carry in Pennsylvania?

We do not state a stop sign point value on this page. The point information we can source for Pennsylvania is speed-based: 2–5 points for 11+ mph over, and 6 points triggers a departmental exam/hearing.

Those tiers describe speeding, so do not read them as the stop sign value. PennDOT is the authority on how a stop sign conviction is recorded (PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services).

Two other facts help show how the system works. Reaching 6 points triggers a departmental exam or hearing. High-speed convictions add a 15-day suspension at 31+ over, which does not apply to a stop sign violation.

To find your point exposure, check your citation and your PennDOT driving record rather than relying on a general table.

Does a stop sign ticket raise my insurance?

It can. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums by about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Pennsylvania rate or a promise about your policy.

A stop sign violation is generally a moving violation, but how your insurer treats it depends on your company, your record and how the case ends. We do not recommend any insurer and cannot predict your premium.

The bigger cost is often over time rather than the fine itself. A conviction can affect your rate at renewal, so the cost of the ticket may extend well beyond the court payment.

  • Ask your insurer how it handles a conviction versus a case that ends without one.
  • Check your renewal notice for any change tied to a violation.
  • Compare the possible premium change with the cost of the ticket when you decide how to respond.

Can a stop sign ticket be dismissed or reduced?

You generally have a choice about how to respond. The citation explains your options and the deadline for responding. Pleading guilty and paying typically ends the case, but it can also mean a conviction on your record.

Pleading not guilty usually leads to a hearing where you can present your side. The officer's account, the location of the sign and the conditions at the intersection can all matter. What outcomes are available depends on the court and your case.

We cannot say how likely a dismissal or reduction is in any court. Because the court sets the amount, anything beyond the citation is a question for that court's office.

  • Read the response instructions on the citation first.
  • Ask the court what options it offers for your violation.
  • For legal advice about your case, speak with a licensed attorney. We do not recommend specific attorneys.

What happens if I don't pay?

Ignoring a citation usually makes things worse. Courts can add costs and take action against your license or your case. The exact steps depend on the court and on Pennsylvania procedure.

Because court costs and statutory fees already tend to exceed the base fine, late action can raise the total further. The citation and the court notice will list the consequences and the deadlines that apply to you.

PennDOT handles license status in Pennsylvania (PennDOT Driver and Vehicle Services). If you are unsure whether anything has been reported on your record, check with PennDOT directly.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court office before the deadline and ask what it allows.
