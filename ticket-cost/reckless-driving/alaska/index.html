How much is a reckless driving ticket in Alaska?

Nationally, reckless driving fines commonly run up to $1,000–$2,500, and the offense is a criminal misdemeanor with possible jail. In Alaska, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Alaska Division of Motor Vehicles).

That means there is no single flat price we can quote for the state. The national range is a guide to scale, not a promise of what you will owe.

The national range is not an Alaska fine. Your citation and the court that issued it control the actual amount.

The fine is also only part of the cost. A criminal case can bring other costs, such as time off work for court dates and higher insurance premiums afterward.

If you want the real number, read the citation itself and then check with the court named on it.

How is the reckless driving fine set in Alaska?

The fine for this violation is set by the court within the state's traffic code. It is not a fixed figure printed in a table that applies to everyone.

Because the court sets the amount, two drivers charged with the same offense can see different outcomes. Details of the stop, the driver's record and the court handling the case can all matter.

Fines can also be higher in certain places. Work-zone and school-zone violations carry doubled fines under the schedule, so where the driving happened can change the cost.

  • The court on the citation sets the exact amount.
  • Work-zone and school-zone violations carry doubled fines.
  • The national range of up to $1,000–$2,500 is context only.

You can read the statutory text itself through Alaska statutes via Justia. Statutes can be amended, so confirm the current version before relying on any summary.

Is reckless driving a criminal charge in Alaska?

The locked facts for this page describe reckless driving as a criminal misdemeanor with possible jail. That sets it apart from an ordinary speeding ticket, which is usually handled as a civil traffic matter.

A criminal charge can mean a court appearance rather than simply mailing in a payment. It can also leave a criminal record, which may matter for jobs, housing and professional licenses.

This page does not list specific jail terms or classes of offense. Those details are in the statute and on your charging document, and we do not state them from memory.

Because the stakes are higher than for a routine ticket, many people choose to talk with a licensed attorney. TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and does not recommend any specific attorney.

How many points does reckless driving add in Alaska?

The state's points system assigns 2–6 demerit points by speed. Under that system, 12 points in 12 months means a license suspension.

Those figures come from the state context we sourced. They do not tell you the exact point value for a reckless driving conviction on your record, because the value depends on how the offense is classified and the facts of the case.

To find the exact count for your situation, check your citation and ask the Alaska Division of Motor Vehicles.

Points add up. If you already have points from other violations, a new conviction can move you closer to the 12-points-in-12-months suspension threshold.

A criminal conviction can also affect your license in ways separate from points. The court or the DMV will tell you if your driving privileges are affected.

Does reckless driving raise insurance rates?

Usually, yes. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not an Alaska-specific one.

Reckless driving is a more serious conviction than many moving violations. Some insurers may react more strongly, but we do not have a sourced figure for that, so we do not state one.

How much your own premium changes depends on your insurer, your driving record and the details of the conviction. A conviction can also affect how long the surcharge lasts, and that varies by company.

  • National average: about 20% for moving-violation convictions.
  • Your actual change depends on your insurer and record.
  • Ask your insurer how a conviction would be treated at renewal.

We do not recommend any specific insurer. Comparing quotes after a conviction is a common step, but that is your decision.

Can a reckless driving charge be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes charges are dismissed or resolved as a lesser offense. Whether that is possible depends on the facts, the evidence, the court and the prosecutor's position.

We cannot tell you whether it is likely in your case, and nothing here is legal advice. We also do not state any particular outcome from memory, because we do not have a sourced figure for how often it happens.

Here are general steps people consider after being cited:

  1. Read the citation closely for the charge, the court and any dates.
  2. Note your deadline to respond or appear.
  3. Consider speaking with a licensed attorney about a criminal misdemeanor charge.
  4. Ask the court how a reduced charge would change the fine, if at all.

A reduced charge may change the fine, the points and the insurance effect. The court sets the amount, so ask the court what applies after any change.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore the citation?

Ignoring a citation is risky, especially when the charge is a criminal misdemeanor with possible jail. Missing a court date can lead to further action by the court.

We do not list specific penalties for non-payment because those are not part of the locked facts for this page. The citation and the court clerk can tell you what applies to your case.

Unresolved matters can also reach your driving record. The Alaska Division of Motor Vehicles handles license status, so check there if you are unsure where you stand.

If you cannot pay the full amount, contact the court before the deadline and ask what options exist. Do not wait until after a missed date.
