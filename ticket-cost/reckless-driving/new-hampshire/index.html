How much is a reckless driving ticket in New Hampshire?

There is no single New Hampshire price tag we can quote. Nationally, fines for reckless driving commonly run up to $1,000–$2,500, and the charge is a criminal misdemeanor with possible jail (New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles). That range is a national picture, not a New Hampshire figure.

In New Hampshire, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation. The number printed on your paperwork, or the amount a judge orders, is the figure that matters for you.

Reckless driving is not a routine speeding-style ticket. Because it is a criminal misdemeanor, the possible consequences go beyond a fine, and the total cost can include more than the amount on the citation.

Below we explain what drives the total, what is added on top of the base fine, and what the charge can mean for your record and insurance.

How is the reckless driving fine set in New Hampshire?

The fine is set by the court within the state's traffic code. We do not publish a New Hampshire dollar figure for it, because the amount comes from the court and the citation rather than from a fixed number we can verify.

Because this is a misdemeanor, the case is handled as a criminal matter. That usually means a court appearance rather than simply mailing in a payment, though your citation and the court's instructions control what you must do.

  • Read the citation. It shows the charge, the court, and any date you must appear.
  • Check the court's instructions. Whether you can pay or must appear depends on the charge and the court.
  • Look up the statute. The traffic code is available through New Hampshire statutes via Justia.

The penalty can also include jail, since the charge is a misdemeanor. Whether that is realistic in a given case is up to the prosecutor and judge.

What is the 24% penalty assessment on a New Hampshire fine?

A 24% penalty assessment is added to every base fine by statute. It is on top of the fine, so the amount you owe is higher than the base fine alone.

For example, if a court sets a base fine, you should expect the assessment to be added to that figure. We do not show a worked dollar example, because that would require a base fine we cannot verify.

  • The base fine is set by the court on the citation.
  • The 24% assessment is added to it.
  • Ask the court clerk for the full total before you pay.

When you budget for the charge, use the total the court gives you, not just the base number on the citation.

How many points is reckless driving in New Hampshire?

New Hampshire's point system assigns 3–4 points by offense. The exact number depends on the specific offense you are convicted of, so check the conviction record or ask the DMV how your case is scored (New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles).

Points matter because they add up. Accumulating 12 points in 12 months means up to a 3-month suspension.

One reckless driving conviction does not reach 12 points by itself. But combined with other violations in the same 12 months, it can move you closer to the suspension threshold.

The suspension rule is stated as "up to" 3 months, so the actual length depends on the circumstances and the DMV's process. Your own record is the best guide to where you stand.

Does reckless driving raise insurance rates?

Usually, yes. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a New Hampshire rate, and we do not claim it applies to your policy.

A reckless driving conviction is more serious than many moving violations, so some insurers may treat it differently. We have no verified figure for that, so the 20% average should be read only as a general benchmark.

  • Rates depend on your insurer, your history, and your policy terms.
  • The increase may last for as long as the conviction affects your rating.
  • Comparing quotes after a conviction is a common step.

We do not recommend any specific insurer. Your insurance company or a licensed agent can tell you how a conviction would affect your rate.

Can a reckless driving charge be dismissed or reduced?

It can happen in some cases, but outcomes depend on the facts, the prosecutor, and the court. We cannot predict whether yours would be dismissed or reduced, and we do not give legal advice.

Because the charge is a criminal misdemeanor with possible jail, many people consider speaking with a licensed attorney before deciding how to respond. We do not recommend any specific attorney.

  • Do not ignore the citation. Missing a required date can create new problems.
  • Ask what your options are. The court clerk can explain procedure, though not legal strategy.
  • Understand the trade-offs. A reduced charge may change the points, the fine, and the insurance effect.

If a charge is reduced, the new offense may carry different points than reckless driving. The point range of 3–4 by offense is the only point figure we can confirm.

What happens if I don't pay or don't show up?

We do not have a verified list of New Hampshire's specific penalties for non-payment, so we will not guess at amounts or deadlines. In general, ignoring a citation tends to make a case worse, not better.

Because reckless driving is a misdemeanor, a missed court date can matter more than it would for a minor ticket. Your citation and the court's notices explain what is required and by when.

  • Contact the court named on the citation as soon as possible.
  • Ask what the total owed is, including the 24% penalty assessment.
  • Keep copies of anything you file or pay.

The DMV can also tell you how an unresolved case may affect your license status (New Hampshire Division of Motor Vehicles).
