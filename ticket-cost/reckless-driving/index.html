How much is a reckless driving ticket?

The headline number for the country is that fines are commonly up to $1,000–$2,500, and reckless driving is a criminal misdemeanor in every state, with possible jail (Governors Highway Safety Association). That is a very different category from an ordinary speeding ticket.

The fine is only one part of the bill. Most courts add costs and surcharges on top, and those vary by state, county and court. We do not publish a single national figure for them because none exists.

Your own total depends on several things:

  • The state statute you were charged under.
  • The county or court that hears the case.
  • Whether the judge orders jail, probation or a driving class in addition to a fine.
  • Your driving record and any prior convictions.
  • The facts of the stop, such as the speed or the conduct alleged.

Because it is a criminal charge, the cost can also include things that never show up on a fine schedule. Lost work time, legal fees and higher insurance premiums often exceed the fine itself. We cover insurance below.

Bottom line: Expect a fine in the range of up to $1,000–$2,500 as a common ceiling, plus court costs that vary by county and court. Read your citation or summons for the exact amount and the court's instructions.

Where a number on this page is not one of the locked figures, we say it varies by state. We would rather leave a gap than guess at a local amount.

How much is reckless driving by state?

Each state writes its own reckless driving statute and sets its own penalties. The two examples we can state with confidence show how wide the gap is.

Virginia. Virginia classes reckless driving, including 20+ over or 86+ mph, as a Class 1 misdemeanor. Fines go up to $2,500, and jail is possible. In Virginia, a speed-based charge can therefore become a criminal case rather than a simple infraction.

California. California's reckless driving statute carries $145–$1,000 and up to 90 days. The range is wide, so the amount a judge sets depends on the facts and the court.

For every other state, the honest answer is that it varies by state. The typical US range of fines commonly up to $1,000–$2,500 is a useful benchmark, but a given state may sit above or below it. The text of each state's code is the controlling source, and many are searchable through state codes via Justia.

When you compare states, keep these points in mind:

  • The same driving can be charged differently in different states.
  • Some states tie reckless driving to a set speed. Virginia does, with 20+ over or 86+ mph.
  • Others define it by conduct, such as willful or wanton disregard for safety.
  • Court costs and surcharges are added separately and vary by county and court.

For a broader view of how states differ on traffic safety laws, the Governors Highway Safety Association keeps a state law directory.

Do not assume a friend's result in another state applies to you. A fine that was dismissed or reduced in one county says little about yours.

Is reckless driving a criminal charge or a traffic ticket?

It is a criminal misdemeanor in every state, with possible jail (Governors Highway Safety Association). You may receive it on a roadside citation that looks like a traffic ticket, but it is handled as a criminal matter.

That has practical consequences. A criminal charge can mean a mandatory court appearance, a different set of rights and a record that is treated differently from an infraction. Virginia and California both show the jail exposure: Virginia's Class 1 misdemeanor carries possible jail, and California's statute allows up to 90 days.

What the law generally looks at varies by state, but there are two common approaches:

  • Conduct-based: driving with willful or wanton disregard for the safety of people or property.
  • Speed-based: a stated speed threshold triggers the charge. Virginia's 20+ over or 86+ mph is the locked example here.

Speed is a major factor in serious crashes. For background on why agencies treat excessive speed so seriously, see NHTSA on speeding and IIHS on speed. These pages explain the safety rationale, not state penalties.

A misdemeanor conviction can also affect matters beyond the court. Some employers, licensing bodies and background checks ask about convictions. The details vary by state and by the type of work, so we do not give specifics here.

TicketCostGuide is not a law firm and cannot tell you how a charge will be treated in your case. Read the statute named on your citation and consider speaking with a licensed attorney in your state.

How many points does reckless driving add to your license?

Where a state uses a points system, typical point values for reckless driving are 4–8. That is the national picture, not a promise for your state.

Not every state has a points system, and those that do set their own values and thresholds. Some states treat a reckless driving conviction as serious enough that it carries consequences beyond points. We do not state those from memory, since they vary by state.

A few things are worth knowing about points:

  • Points usually attach to a conviction, not to the citation alone.
  • A plea to a different charge may carry different points, or none.
  • Points can stay on a driving record for a period that varies by state.
  • Accumulating points can trigger separate actions by the licensing agency.

If you want to know the exact value, look up your state's point schedule through its motor vehicle agency, and check the statute through state codes via Justia. The statute and the agency's schedule control, not a general guide like this one.

The 4–8 range sits at the heavier end of point schedules, which reflects how seriously states treat the offense. Even so, points are often a smaller cost than the insurance effect discussed next.

Remember: Typical point values of 4–8 apply only where systems exist. Your state may use a different scale or none at all.

Does reckless driving raise your insurance?

Yes, often by a lot. Insurance increases of 60%+ are common, according to national averages. Your actual change depends on your insurer, your state, your record and how the conviction is reported.

This is a national average and not a quote. Some drivers see a smaller increase, and some see more or have their policy non-renewed. We cannot predict your premium, and we do not recommend any insurer.

Why the effect is large:

  • Insurers treat reckless driving as a serious violation, in part because it is a criminal misdemeanor.
  • The conviction may stay on your record and affect pricing for an extended time that varies by state and insurer.
  • A speed-based charge, like Virginia's 20+ over or 86+ mph, signals high risk to underwriters.

The research on speed and crash risk explains why insurers weigh it heavily. See IIHS on speed for the safety data behind it.

Because of this, the cheapest outcome in court is not always the one with the lowest fine. A reduction to a lesser offense may cost more in fines or fees but protect you from a long period of higher premiums. That trade-off is worth discussing with a licensed attorney in your state.

A 60%+ increase on an annual premium can add up to more than the fine. Compare total cost over time, not just the amount due to the court.

Can a reckless driving ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes. Outcomes vary by state, county, court and the facts of the stop. We cannot promise any result, and nothing here is legal advice.

Common paths people ask about include:

  • Dismissal: the case is dropped, for example if the evidence is weak or a procedural problem exists.
  • Reduction: the charge is amended to a lesser offense. This can change the fine, the points and how insurers see it.
  • Diversion or a class: some courts allow a driving course or conditions in exchange for a better outcome. Availability varies by court.
  • Contesting at trial: you plead not guilty and the prosecution must prove each element.

Because reckless driving is a criminal misdemeanor in every state, with possible jail, the stakes are higher than for a routine ticket. Virginia's Class 1 misdemeanor and California's statute with up to 90 days show the jail exposure at the top end.

Many people in this position speak with a licensed attorney in their state before entering a plea. We do not recommend any specific attorney. Your state bar or local court may offer referral information.

Before any hearing, it helps to do the following:

  1. Read the citation and note the statute and court listed.
  2. Check the court's instructions on how and when to respond.
  3. Look up the statute text, for example through state codes via Justia.
  4. Gather records, such as photos, dashcam footage or the dates and times involved.

Do not assume a reduction is available. Whether a prosecutor or judge will agree depends on your state, your record and the facts.

What happens if I don't pay or ignore a reckless driving charge?

Ignoring it usually makes things worse. Because reckless driving is a criminal misdemeanor in every state, with possible jail, missing a court date can lead to additional consequences. The exact steps vary by state, county and court.

Depending on where you are, a missed appearance may lead to:

  • A warrant or similar court order.
  • Added fees or penalties on top of the original amount.
  • Action against your driving privileges by the licensing agency.
  • Collection efforts on an unpaid fine.

We do not state specific deadlines or fee amounts, because they vary by court and are not part of our locked figures. Your citation or summons should show how and when to respond.

Paying a fine in a criminal case can also be treated as a guilty plea, depending on the court. That matters for points and insurance. Before you pay, confirm what payment means in your case. If the instructions are unclear, call the court clerk and ask.

If you cannot pay, many courts have processes for payment plans or hardship requests. Availability varies by court, so ask the clerk early rather than waiting until a deadline passes.

Tip: Contact the court named on your citation as soon as you can. Early contact leaves more options open than silence.
