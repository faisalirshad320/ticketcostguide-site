How much does a reckless driving ticket cost in Massachusetts?

Nationally, reckless driving fines commonly run up to $1,000–$2,500, and the offense is a criminal misdemeanor with possible jail. In Massachusetts, the exact amount is set by the court on the citation (Massachusetts Registry of Motor Vehicles).

Primary figure: Nationally fines commonly up to $1,000–$2,500; a criminal misdemeanor with possible jail; in Massachusetts the exact amount is set by the court on the citation.

We do not publish a single Massachusetts dollar amount, because the court decides it within the state's traffic code. The national range is a guide to scale, not a quote for your case.

The fine is only one part of the total cost. Depending on your case, you may also face:

  • Court-assessed fines and any surcharges listed on the citation or court notice
  • Higher insurance premiums after a conviction
  • Time away from work for court dates
  • Possible attorney fees if you hire one

The citation and any paperwork from the court are the most reliable place to find your actual amount.

How is the reckless driving fine set in Massachusetts?

In Massachusetts, the court sets the amount on the citation, within the state's traffic code. There is no fixed price list we can point to for this offense.

Because reckless driving is treated nationally as a criminal misdemeanor rather than a simple infraction, the outcome depends on the court's handling of the case. That is different from a routine speeding ticket, where the amount is often easier to predict.

Factors that can matter in a court's decision include the facts alleged in the citation, your driving history, and how the case is resolved. We cannot say how any of them would change the figure in your case.

You can read the state's traffic code through Massachusetts statutes via Justia. For licensing questions, the Massachusetts Registry of Motor Vehicles is the official source.

Because the amount is set by the court on the citation, it can differ from court to court. Do not rely on a figure quoted for another court.

Are there extra surcharges or court costs?

Our sourced facts include one Massachusetts surcharge: a $50 head-injury surcharge applies to every speeding conviction (Massachusetts Registry of Motor Vehicles).

That fact is stated for speeding convictions. We do not have a sourced statement that it applies to a reckless driving conviction, so do not assume it does or does not.

Other court costs or fees may appear on your citation or court notice. We do not list amounts for them, because we have no sourced figures.

  • Read every line of the citation or court notice.
  • Ask the court clerk what the total due would be if you resolve the case.
  • Keep a copy of anything you pay.

How many points does reckless driving add in Massachusetts?

Massachusetts uses insurance surcharge points (SDIP) rather than license suspension points for ordinary speeding. Under that system, 3 surchargeable events in 24 months require a driver retraining course, and 7 in 3 years result in suspension (Massachusetts Registry of Motor Vehicles).

Those thresholds are stated for ordinary speeding. We do not have a sourced point value for reckless driving, so we do not give one.

Treat the thresholds as a warning about how events add up. Each surchargeable event counts toward the 24-month and 3-year totals.

To see how a specific conviction would be recorded, check your driving record or ask the Registry of Motor Vehicles.

Does reckless driving raise insurance rates?

Usually, yes. Moving-violation convictions typically raise US premiums about 20% on average. That is a national figure, not a Massachusetts quote.

Your own change could be higher or lower. It depends on your insurer, your history, and how the conviction is recorded. We cannot say how any insurer would treat reckless driving.

Massachusetts uses insurance surcharge points (SDIP), which is the system that links violations to premiums. Over time, a conviction can cost more in premiums than the court fine itself.

  • Ask your insurer how a conviction might affect renewal, without giving them more detail than necessary.
  • Compare quotes before and after any change in your record.
  • Keep records of how your outcome was reported.

Is reckless driving a crime, and can it mean jail?

Nationally, reckless driving is a criminal misdemeanor with possible jail. That sets it apart from an ordinary civil traffic ticket.

A criminal charge can carry consequences beyond money. It may appear on a record, and it may affect licensing or insurance.

In Massachusetts, the court sets the outcome on the citation. We do not state a jail term, because we have no sourced figure for one.

Because possible jail is part of the national description, take the citation seriously and read it closely. Note the court, the date, and what you are charged with.

Can a reckless driving ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes a case ends in a dismissal or a lesser charge, but we cannot say how often, or whether yours would. We have no sourced data on outcomes, so we do not offer odds.

The options usually discussed are:

  • Pay and accept the outcome. This resolves the case, but a conviction can affect your record and insurance.
  • Contest the charge. This means going before the court and presenting your side.
  • Seek a different resolution. The court or prosecutor may offer alternatives. Whether one is available depends on the court and your case.

Weigh the fine against the longer-term costs, including the national average of about 20% higher premiums after a moving-violation conviction.

Since reckless driving is a criminal misdemeanor with possible jail, many people choose to speak with a licensed attorney before deciding. We do not recommend a particular one.

What happens if I ignore or don't pay the ticket?

Ignoring a reckless driving citation is risky, because it is a criminal matter in the national description. Missing a required court date or payment can make your situation worse.

We do not have sourced figures for late fees, warrants or license actions in Massachusetts, so we do not state any. The citation and the court's notice should say what is required and by when.

  1. Find the court and the date listed on the citation.
  2. Contact the court clerk if anything is unclear.
  3. Do not skip a required appearance.
  4. Keep proof of anything you file or pay.

For license questions, the Massachusetts Registry of Motor Vehicles is the official source.
