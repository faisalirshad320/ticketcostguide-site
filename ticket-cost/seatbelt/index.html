How much is a seatbelt ticket in the US?

The US seatbelt fine range is $10–$200 by state for adults (Governors Highway Safety Association). That is a wide spread, and where you are stopped matters far more than almost any other factor.

The low end is closer to a nuisance fee. The high end is a real hit to a monthly budget. Both are small compared with many moving-violation fines, which is one reason seatbelt tickets are often treated as minor offenses.

Three things shape the number on your citation:

  • The state schedule. Each state sets its own base fine in its statutes or in a court fine schedule.
  • Assessments and court costs. Some states add surcharges on top of the base fine, so the amount you pay can be well above the number written in the statute.
  • Who was unbuckled. This page covers adults. Rules for children and for people in rear seats can be different, and we do not publish figures for them here.

Because local courts and counties can add their own costs, the amount on your ticket is the one that counts. If the ticket lists a fine and a separate total due, the total due is what you would owe if you simply paid it.

Quick take: the national range is $10–$200 by state for adults. Anything more specific than that depends on your state and, in some places, your court. We do not guess at exact local amounts.

The sections below walk through state examples, points, insurance, enforcement type and dismissal options. Each figure comes from the sources listed at the bottom of the page.

How much is a seatbelt ticket in Texas, New York and California?

Three states give a useful picture of how different the same offense can look. These are the only state-specific figures we publish on this page, taken from state statutes and schedules (State codes via Justia).

  • Texas: seatbelt fines are $25–$50.
  • New York: the adult fine is up to $50, and there are no points for adult front-seat violations.
  • California: totals reach ≈$160 with assessments.

Notice what the California number includes. The base fine is only part of the story, because assessments are added on top. That is why two drivers in different states can both be cited under a seatbelt law and end up paying very different totals even when the base fines look similar.

Notice also that Texas and New York are stated as ranges or maximums. A range means the exact amount can depend on the court or the circumstances. An “up to” figure means the fine can be lower than the number shown. In both cases, check the citation or the court's payment page for the exact amount due.

If your state is not listed here, the national range of $10–$200 by state for adults is the safest guide we can give. We would rather say an amount varies by state than print a number we cannot trace to a source.

Do not treat these examples as a prediction for your ticket. They show how wide the spread is, and they show that assessments can matter as much as the base fine.

For the exact wording of your state's law, state codes are published online. You can start from the state code index on Justia and look for the section on safety belts or occupant restraints.

How many points is a seatbelt ticket?

In most states, an adult seatbelt ticket is a no-point offense. New York is a clear example: there are no points for adult front-seat violations.

That matters because points, not fines, are what usually drive license consequences. A ticket that carries no points generally does not move you toward a suspension threshold or a driver-improvement requirement the way a moving violation can.

There are two cautions.

  • “Most” is not “all.” Some states may handle seatbelt violations differently. We do not publish point values for any state other than the New York no-point rule above, so check your own state's statute or DMV rules.
  • The ticket may include other violations. If you were cited for a seatbelt violation along with something else, such as a moving violation, the other citation may carry points even when the seatbelt charge does not.

When you read your citation, look at each line separately. Each charge is its own offense with its own fine and, in some states, its own point treatment.

Also check whether the violation involves an adult in the front seat or another type of passenger. The New York no-point rule we cite applies to adult front-seat violations, and we do not extend it to other situations.

Short version: most states treat adult seatbelt tickets as no-point. Confirm with your state's DMV or the court clerk before assuming that applies to you.

If you are unsure how points work where you live, the state code index on Justia is a place to read the statute itself rather than relying on a summary.

Does a seatbelt ticket raise insurance rates?

Often, no. Insurers often don't surcharge adult seatbelt tickets, which fits with the fact that most states treat them as no-point offenses.

Insurers set rates using their own rules and filings, and those rules differ by company and by state. We cannot tell you what your insurer will do, and we do not publish surcharge percentages or dollar amounts because we have no locked source for them.

Here is how to think about the risk:

  • No points usually means less rating impact. Many insurers look at points or at moving-violation records. A no-point seatbelt ticket may never show up in the way a speeding ticket would.
  • A seatbelt ticket can still be on your record. Even when it carries no points, the citation or conviction can exist in court records.
  • Other tickets change the picture. If the stop also produced a moving violation, that violation is the more likely cause of any rate change.

If you want a firm answer, ask your insurer or agent how they treat a non-moving, no-point violation. Asking in general terms does not commit you to anything.

TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer or comparison service, and nothing on this page is insurance advice.

Bottom line: insurers often don't surcharge adult seatbelt tickets, but it depends on the company and your state. Paying the fine on time is usually the cleaner path, because an unpaid ticket can create bigger problems (see below).

If the ticket is in a state where seatbelt violations are treated differently from the usual no-point approach, check how that state reports convictions to insurers and to the DMV.

What is the difference between primary and secondary seatbelt enforcement?

Primary vs secondary enforcement varies by state, and it decides whether you can be stopped for not wearing a belt at all.

  • Primary enforcement means an officer can stop you just for an unbuckled seatbelt.
  • Secondary enforcement means an officer generally needs another reason to stop you first, such as a separate traffic violation, before citing you for the belt.

This is one reason the Governors Highway Safety Association tracks seatbelt laws state by state (Governors Highway Safety Association). The same behavior can lead to a ticket in one state and only a lecture in another.

Why it matters for your ticket:

  • If your state uses secondary enforcement, ask what the original reason for the stop was. A ticket that depends on a valid stop can be harder to sustain if the stop itself was not lawful.
  • If your state uses primary enforcement, the stop for the belt alone is normally allowed, and the argument shifts to the facts of whether you were belted.

We do not list which states are primary and which are secondary here, because those classifications can change when legislatures amend their laws. Use the GHSA state laws page to check the current status for your state, and read the statute itself for the exact language.

Enforcement type can also vary by who is in the vehicle and where they sit. Do not assume the rule for a front-seat adult applies to every seat.

Check your state's enforcement type before deciding how to respond. It affects whether the stop itself can be questioned.

Can a seatbelt ticket be dismissed or reduced?

Sometimes. Whether it can depends on your state, your court and the facts. We cannot promise any outcome, and this is general information, not legal advice.

Options people commonly look into include:

  • Contesting the ticket. You can plead not guilty and ask for a hearing. The officer generally has to show you were not wearing a belt when stopped.
  • Challenging the stop. In states with secondary enforcement, the question of why you were stopped can matter.
  • Asking about reduced or alternative outcomes. Some courts may offer a reduction, a diversion option or a way to resolve the ticket with a lower penalty. Availability varies by court, so ask the clerk what is allowed for your citation.
  • Showing a legitimate exemption. State laws can include exemptions, for example for certain medical situations or vehicle types. Read your state's statute to see whether one applies, and ask the court what documentation it accepts.

Before you decide, compare the cost of fighting it with the cost of paying. The fine range of $10–$200 by state for adults means that in some states the fine is small. Taking time off work to appear may cost more than the ticket itself.

If the amount is high, or if the ticket is paired with other charges, you may want to speak with a licensed attorney in your state. We do not recommend any particular attorney.

Read the back of the citation. It usually explains how to plead, where to pay and what happens if you want a hearing. Follow those instructions exactly, because court deadlines are strict.

What happens if I don't pay a seatbelt ticket?

Ignoring a seatbelt ticket usually makes the problem bigger than the original fine. We do not publish exact deadlines or late-fee amounts, because they vary by state and court.

Common consequences of leaving a ticket unpaid include:

  • Late fees or added costs. Courts can add charges to the original amount.
  • A default or failure-to-appear notation. This can create a separate problem on top of the seatbelt charge.
  • License or registration holds. Some states can place a hold on a license or registration for unresolved tickets.
  • A warrant in some places. In some jurisdictions, failing to appear or pay can lead to further court action.

A no-point ticket can still turn into a license problem if it goes unpaid. The points rule does not protect you from the court's collection process.

What to do instead:

  1. Read the citation for the due date and the instructions for paying or contesting.
  2. Contact the court clerk if you cannot pay the full amount at once, and ask whether a payment plan or extension is possible.
  3. If you plan to contest the ticket, respond within the deadline and keep a copy of anything you file.

If you missed the deadline, contact the court right away. Clerks can often tell you what your options are, and acting early is usually better than waiting.

Remember that amounts vary by state, and by court costs and assessments in some places. Confirm the total due with the court that issued your ticket.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a seatbelt ticket?

For adults, seatbelt fines run $10–$200 by state. Examples: Texas is $25–$50, New York's adult fine is up to $50, and California's totals reach about $160 with assessments. The exact amount depends on your state and sometimes on court costs, so check your citation.

Does a seatbelt ticket add points to your license?

Most states treat adult seatbelt tickets as no-point. In New York, adult front-seat violations carry no points. Because rules vary by state, check your own state's statute or DMV guidance, and look at any other charges on the same ticket separately.

Will a seatbelt ticket raise my car insurance?

Often it won't. Insurers often don't surcharge adult seatbelt tickets, and most states treat them as no-point. Policies and filings differ by company and state, so ask your insurer how it treats a no-point violation. Other tickets from the same stop could matter more.

How much is a seatbelt ticket in Texas?

Texas seatbelt fines are $25–$50. The exact amount on your ticket can depend on the court and the circumstances, so confirm the total due with the court named on your citation before paying or deciding whether to contest it.

How much is a seatbelt ticket in California?

California's seatbelt totals reach about $160 with assessments. The assessments are added on top of the base fine, which is why the total is higher than the fine alone. Check your citation or the court's payment page for the exact amount you owe.

Can police pull you over just for not wearing a seatbelt?

It depends on the state. Primary vs secondary enforcement varies by state. Under primary enforcement, an officer can stop you for the belt alone. Under secondary enforcement, there generally must be another reason for the stop first. Check your state's law through the GHSA state laws page.

Can I get a seatbelt ticket dismissed?

Sometimes, depending on your state, court and the facts. You can contest the ticket, question the stop, or ask the clerk about reduced or alternative outcomes. We cannot promise a result. For a high fine or multiple charges, consider speaking with a licensed attorney in your state.
