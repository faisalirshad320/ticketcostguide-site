How much is a speeding ticket in the US?

For ordinary speeds, a speeding ticket usually totals roughly $50–$500 in most states. That range runs from under $20 in the lowest fixed-fee states to $900+ with assessments in the highest, depending on state, mph over and zone (Governors Highway Safety Association).

That is a very wide spread for the same basic offense. The reason is that there is no national fine schedule. Each state, and sometimes each court, decides how the number is built.

The figure printed on a citation may also be only part of what you pay. Surcharges, assessments and court costs can sit on top of the base fine, and in some states they are larger than the fine itself.

Four things move the total more than anything else:

  • The state where you were cited, and how it prices speeding.
  • How far over the limit you were going, in mph.
  • The zone, such as a school or work zone, where fines are commonly doubled.
  • Added assessments and special fees that apply on top of the base fine.

How are speeding ticket fines set?

Fines come from state statutes and the fine schedules that courts use to apply them. The legislature usually sets the rules, and the court or a statewide schedule turns those rules into a dollar amount for your ticket.

In practice, states use a few different methods. Knowing which one applies to you explains most of the price difference.

  • Fixed fees. One set amount per offense or per band of speed. North Dakota's fixed fees start around $5.
  • Per-mph formulas. The fine grows with every mile per hour over the limit. Pennsylvania uses a $35 + $2-per-mph formula.
  • Statutory tiers. Lawmakers set bands of speed with a set fine in each band. Florida sets uniform statutory tiers of $25–$250.
  • Base fine plus assessments. A low base fine is multiplied by add-ons. California base fines of $35–$200 become roughly $238–$900 in totals once penalty assessments are applied.
  • Court discretion within a range. A judge picks a figure up to a ceiling. Georgia's court fine runs from $0 to $1,000.

Some states mix these methods. A tier or formula may set the starting point, and surcharges then change the final bill.

Speed limits themselves are also set in different ways. States set limits as absolute or presumed ('prima facie') limits, which affects how a ticket can be written and argued.

Why do speeding ticket totals differ so much between states?

The table above shows five states that price speeding in five different ways. None of them is typical on its own, but together they show why a single national average can mislead.

California is the clearest example of the base-fine problem. Base fines of $35–$200 become roughly $238–$900 in totals once penalty assessments are added. The number a driver first hears and the number they pay can be far apart.

Georgia combines a wide court fine of $0–$1,000 with a separate $200 'Super Speeder' fee. That fee applies at 75+ mph on two-lane roads or 85+ mph anywhere, as the locked state schedule lists it as 75+/85+ mph, and it is added on top of the court fine.

Pennsylvania uses a formula of $35 + $2 per mph. The fine rises predictably as the speed rises, so the amount depends heavily on how far over the limit the driver was recorded.

Florida uses uniform statutory tiers of $25–$250. These double in school and work zones, so location matters as much as speed.

North Dakota sits at the low end. Its fixed fees start around $5, which is why the lowest end of the national range is under $20.

These are examples, not a full 50-state list. If your state is not shown, look up its current fine schedule or read the citation itself.

What surcharges and fees get added to a speeding ticket?

The base fine is often only the start. Many states add assessments, surcharges or special fees that are written into statute and applied automatically.

California is the best-known case. Its base fines of $35–$200 become roughly $238–$900 in totals with penalty assessments. The add-ons are not optional and are not a judge's choice.

Georgia shows a different kind of add-on. A $200 'Super Speeder' fee applies at 75+/85+ mph, on top of a court fine that can range from $0 to $1,000. This is a targeted fee for very high speeds rather than a general surcharge.

Beyond these examples, the exact mix of surcharges and court costs can vary by county and court. We do not list a local figure because the sources we use do not support one. Your citation or the court's payment page should itemize what you owe.

When you compare totals, check what is included:

  • Is the number the base fine only, or the full amount due?
  • Are assessments or surcharges listed separately?
  • Does a special fee apply because of speed or zone?
  • Are there court or processing costs added by the local court?

A ticket that looks cheap at the base-fine level can end up costing far more once the add-ons are included.

Do school and work zones cost more, and how do speed limits work?

Yes, in many places. Fines are commonly doubled in school and work zones. Florida is a clear locked example: its uniform statutory tiers of $25–$250 double in school and work zones.

The doubling is the reason the same speed can produce very different bills. Going a certain number of miles over the limit on an open road is priced one way, and doing it where children or road crews are present is priced another.

It also helps to understand the kind of limit you were charged under. States set limits as absolute or presumed ('prima facie') limits.

  • Absolute limits make exceeding the posted number an offense by itself.
  • Presumed (prima facie) limits treat a higher speed as evidence of unreasonable driving, which a driver may be able to rebut by showing the speed was safe for conditions.

Which type your state uses can shape how a ticket is written and how it is handled in court. It does not change the fact that the fine schedule and any zone rules decide the dollar amount.

For general background on why speed enforcement matters, see the federal overview from NHTSA and the research summaries at IIHS. Neither publishes a fine schedule for your state.

Does a speeding ticket raise insurance rates?

It can. Published market analyses show that a speeding conviction raises US premiums roughly 20–25% on average.

That is an average, not a promise. Your own change depends on your insurer, your driving record and your state, and we do not publish a dollar figure for it because the sources do not support one.

The important point is that the insurance effect often comes from the conviction, not from the ticket itself. How a case ends, and whether it is recorded as a conviction, can change what shows up on your record.

This is why the fine alone understates the cost. A modest fine can be followed by higher premiums that last well beyond the day you pay.

When comparing outcomes, think in two parts:

  • The one-time cost: fine, surcharges and court costs.
  • The ongoing cost: a possible premium increase of roughly 20–25% on average after a conviction.

TicketCostGuide does not recommend any insurer. To see how a ticket would affect your own policy, ask your insurance company or agent directly.

Can a speeding ticket be dismissed or reduced, and what if I don't pay?

Whether a ticket can be dismissed, reduced or resolved another way depends on your state, your court and the facts of your case. We cannot tell you what is available to you, and this page is not legal advice.

What we can say is that the fine is not always final. Because many states price speeding with ranges, tiers or formulas, the exact amount can depend on the speed recorded and on what the court decides.

Georgia is a useful example. Its court fine runs from $0 to $1,000, which shows how much room a court can have within a statute. The separate $200 'Super Speeder' fee at 75+/85+ mph is a different matter, because it is a fee tied to the speed.

If you want to respond to a ticket, start with the paperwork:

  1. Read the citation for the amount, the court and the deadline to respond.
  2. Check the court's website or the contact on the notice for the options it offers.
  3. Confirm whether the amount shown is the full total or just the base fine.
  4. If you are unsure about your rights, consider speaking with a licensed attorney in your state.

Not paying or not responding usually makes the situation worse, not better. The consequences vary by state and court, so check the notice you received rather than guessing. We do not state specific penalties because they differ too widely to summarize with a single sourced figure.

Frequently asked questions

How much is the average speeding ticket?

Most states land at roughly $50–$500 total for ordinary speeds. The spread runs from under $20 in the lowest fixed-fee states to $900+ with assessments in the highest. The exact amount depends on your state, how many mph over the limit you were going, and the zone where you were cited.

Why is a speeding ticket so much more expensive in some states?

States build fines differently. California adds penalty assessments, so base fines of $35–$200 become roughly $238–$900 in totals. Georgia can add a $200 'Super Speeder' fee on top of a court fine of $0–$1,000. Other states use fixed fees or simple formulas that keep totals lower.

Are speeding fines higher in school zones?

Often, yes. Fines are commonly doubled in school and work zones. In Florida, the uniform statutory tiers of $25–$250 double in those zones. Check your state's rules and your citation, because the exact treatment of zones differs by state.

How does Pennsylvania calculate a speeding fine?

Pennsylvania uses a formula of $35 + $2 per mph over the limit. That means the fine rises with each additional mile per hour recorded above the limit. Other costs may be added by the court, so check your citation for the full amount you owe.

What is Georgia's Super Speeder fee?

Georgia adds a $200 'Super Speeder' fee at 75+/85+ mph. It is charged on top of the court fine, which can range from $0 to $1,000. Because the court fine varies, the total depends on the speed recorded and on the court that handles the case.

Does a speeding ticket raise my car insurance?

A speeding conviction raises US premiums roughly 20–25% on average, according to published market analyses. Your own change depends on your insurer, record and state. To learn how a ticket would affect your policy, ask your insurance company directly.

What is the difference between absolute and prima facie speed limits?

States set limits as absolute or presumed ('prima facie') limits. An absolute limit makes going over the posted number an offense in itself. A presumed limit treats a higher speed as evidence of unsafe driving, which may be challenged. Which applies depends on your state.
