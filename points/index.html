How does a license point system work?

A license point system gives each moving violation a number of points. The state adds those points to your driving record after a conviction, and the running total decides whether the state takes action against your license.

The idea is simple: one ticket is a minor event, but repeated tickets show a pattern. Points let a state measure that pattern without reviewing every case by hand.

Across point states, the pattern looks similar. Most point states suspend a license around 8–15 points within 12–24 months; typical moving violations carry 2–6 points (Governors Highway Safety Association). That is the national shape. Each state sets its own exact numbers, so your state's DMV publication is the final word.

Three pieces make up every system:

  • Point values: how many points each type of violation carries.
  • A time window: how long points count toward your total, such as the 12–24 months above.
  • A threshold: the total at which the state suspends or revokes your driving privilege.

Points are assigned by the state licensing agency, not by the judge or the officer. A police officer writes the citation, the court records the outcome, and the DMV applies the points once there is a conviction.

How many points does a typical ticket carry?

Typical moving violations carry 2–6 points (Governors Highway Safety Association). That is a range across point states, not a rule for any one state.

In general, states place less serious violations at the low end of the scale and more dangerous conduct at the high end. Speeding is a common example. Federal and industry safety research on speed, such as that from NHTSA and IIHS, is one reason states treat speeding as a point-worthy offense and often scale points to how far over the limit a driver was.

Do not assume your ticket carries a particular number just because a friend's did. Point values can differ for these reasons:

  • The state may assign different points to the same speed over the limit.
  • Some states give extra points for school zones or work zones.
  • Some states count out-of-state convictions and some do not.
  • Your ticket may be reduced to a different offense with fewer points, or to a non-moving offense with none.

Fine amounts also vary by county or court, and this page does not give a fine figure. Points and fines are separate: the court sets the fine, and the DMV sets the points.

The citation or your state DMV's point schedule will list the value for your specific offense. If the schedule and a website disagree, the schedule governs.

How many points will get my license suspended?

The national range is about 8–15 points within 12–24 months. Individual states fall at different places inside that range, or use an entirely different scale.

These are the locked state examples we track (see the table above):

  • Georgia suspends at 15 points in 24 months.
  • New York suspends at 11 points in 18 months.
  • New Jersey suspends at 12 points.
  • Maryland suspends at 8 points and revokes at 12.
  • California's NOTS acts at 4 points in 12 months. California's points are smaller per offense, so the lower number is not directly comparable to other states.
  • Utah uses a 35–75-points-per-violation scale, with suspension at 200 points in 3 years.

The key lesson is that you cannot compare thresholds across states without also comparing point values. A low threshold paired with small per-offense points can be just as forgiving as a high threshold with large ones. Utah's scale looks huge, but it is built on much larger per-violation numbers.

Maryland's two-step structure is also worth noting. Suspension at 8 and revocation at 12 means the consequence can escalate as points accumulate, rather than a single cutoff applying.

Time windows matter as much as totals. Points that fall outside the window, such as 24 months in Georgia or 18 months in New York, generally stop counting toward a suspension.

Suspension and revocation are not identical in many states. A suspension is usually temporary, while a revocation can require you to apply for a new license. Your DMV explains the difference for your state.

Which states have no point system at all?

Some states do not run a point count. In these places, license action follows convictions directly. The state looks at what you were convicted of, not at a running total.

The no-point states we track are:

  • Hawaii
  • Kansas
  • Louisiana
  • Minnesota
  • Mississippi
  • Oregon
  • Rhode Island (no running point system)
  • Texas (since 2019)
  • Washington
  • Wyoming

Massachusetts is a separate case. It uses insurance-surcharge points (SDIP) instead of a license-suspension point scale.

No points does not mean no consequences. A conviction still goes on your driving record, the court still sets a fine, and the state can still act on your license after certain offenses or repeated convictions. The difference is the mechanism: there is no number to watch climb toward a cutoff.

If you live in one of these states, the practical question changes. Instead of asking how many points you have, ask which convictions trigger license action and how your state's DMV treats repeat offenses. The state DMV publication is the place to confirm this.

Texas is worth a specific note: it appears on this list as having no point system since 2019. If you are reading older material that describes Texas points, it may be out of date.

Does a ticket raise my insurance?

Points on your license and your insurance price are related but not the same thing. The DMV assigns license points. Your insurer decides its own pricing, and this page does not recommend any insurer.

Massachusetts shows the connection most clearly. It uses insurance-surcharge points (SDIP) instead of a license point scale, so the points are built around what you pay for coverage rather than a suspension threshold.

In other states, a conviction on your record can be visible to insurers whether or not your state counts points. That means a no-point state can still have insurance consequences after a conviction, and a point state can have them even if you stay below the suspension threshold.

We do not publish a surcharge amount here because the locked data for this page contains no dollar figures. Surcharge size varies by insurer and by state, and any specific number would need a source we have not cited.

To find out how a ticket could affect your own rates, you can ask your insurer how it treats convictions and whether your state limits what it can do. That is a question for the insurer, not for a general guide like this one.

Fine, points and insurance are three separate costs of one ticket. Look at each one on its own before deciding how to respond.

Can points be avoided, reduced, or dismissed?

Often the only way to avoid points is to avoid a conviction. Because points are applied after a conviction, what happens in court matters more than the number on the schedule.

Common paths people look into include:

  • Contesting the ticket: if the case is dismissed or you are found not responsible, there is no conviction and no points.
  • A reduced charge: some courts or prosecutors allow a plea to a lesser offense, which may carry fewer points or none.
  • Driver improvement or defensive driving courses: some states let these keep a conviction off your record or reduce points. Availability and limits differ by state.

Whether any of these are available depends on your state, your county or court, your driving record and the offense. We cannot say what is available to you, and we do not give legal advice or recommend attorneys.

Read your citation carefully. It usually lists deadlines and instructions for how to respond, and the court clerk can explain the process. If the stakes are high, such as a license already near a threshold, consider speaking with a licensed attorney you choose yourself.

One caution: paying a ticket without contesting it is typically treated as a guilty plea. That can mean a conviction and points. Understand that before you pay.

What happens if I ignore a ticket or reach the threshold?

Ignoring a ticket usually makes things worse. Courts can add penalties, and many states can suspend a license for failing to pay or failing to appear, even if the underlying offense carried few points. The exact steps differ by state and court.

If you reach your state's suspension threshold, the DMV generally sends a notice. A suspension of your driving privilege follows unless your state offers a way to contest or reduce the action. Maryland's example shows how this can escalate: suspension at 8 points, revocation at 12.

In a no-point state, the trigger is different. License action follows convictions directly, so a particular conviction, or a failure to resolve a ticket, can itself lead to action.

Practical steps if you are close to a threshold:

  1. Request your driving record from your state DMV to see your current total and when each item ages off.
  2. Check the time window, such as 12 months in California's NOTS or 24 months in Georgia, so you know when points stop counting.
  3. Respond to every citation by its deadline.
  4. Ask the court or DMV what options exist before a suspension takes effect.

Driving on a suspended license is a separate and often more serious problem. If your license is suspended, confirm your status with the DMV before you drive.

Frequently asked questions

How many points suspend a driver's license?

It depends on the state. Most point states suspend a license around 8–15 points within 12–24 months. Georgia suspends at 15 points in 24 months, New York at 11 points in 18 months, New Jersey at 12 points, and Maryland suspends at 8 and revokes at 12.

How many points is a speeding ticket?

Typical moving violations carry 2–6 points, according to state DMV publications. The exact value for speeding depends on your state and often on how far over the limit you were. Your citation or state DMV point schedule lists the figure for your specific offense.

Which states do not use a point system?

Hawaii, Kansas, Louisiana, Minnesota, Mississippi, Oregon, Rhode Island (no running point system), Texas (since 2019), Washington and Wyoming. In these states, license action follows convictions directly. Massachusetts uses insurance-surcharge points (SDIP) instead of a license point scale.

Why does California suspend at only 4 points?

California's NOTS acts at 4 points in 12 months, but its points are smaller per offense. A lower threshold paired with smaller point values is not directly comparable to states with higher thresholds. Compare the point value and the threshold together, not just the cutoff.

How does Utah's point system work?

Utah uses a 35–75-points-per-violation scale, with suspension at 200 points in 3 years. The numbers look large next to other states because each violation is worth far more points. The scale is simply different, not harsher by default.

Do points go away on their own?

Points count within a time window, such as 12–24 months in most point states. Georgia uses 24 months and New York 18. Once points fall outside the window they generally stop counting toward suspension. Your state DMV can tell you when each item ages off your record.
